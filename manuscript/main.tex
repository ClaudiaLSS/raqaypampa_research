\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract
Accurate household demand estimation is decisive for the technical and financial
viability of off-grid electrification, yet conventional models rely on
urban-centric, income-driven assumptions that misrepresent the culturally
embedded routines of remote rural and Indigenous communities. This paper
develops and tests a mixed-methods pipeline that operationalizes qualitative
socio-cultural knowledge into the quantitative boundary conditions of a
stochastic demand simulation. Drawing on a convergent case study in the
Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory of Raqaypampa, Bolivia---combining 50
semi-structured interviews, socioeconomic and practice surveys of 100
households, and continuous datalogger telemetry from 14 Solar Home
Systems---we extract seventeen rigid socio-technical rules governing daily
energy use and consolidate them into four Energy Behavior Profiles within the
Energy Cultures Framework. These profiles are translated into ``Virtual
Appliances'': behaviour-specific loads bounded by empirically derived time
windows, activation probabilities, and a qualitative rigidity classification.
Through an A/B validation against telemetry, we compare a conventional
survey-only model against the socio-technical model. The socio-technical
parametrization more faithfully reproduces the chronological shape of daily
routines, the cross-profile ordering of day-to-day irregularity, and aggregate
daily energy---properties that a single community-average profile cannot
express by construction, and that bear directly on system sizing and
demand-side planning. We argue that in data-scarce, culturally specific
off-grid settings, qualitative understanding is not a supplement to
quantitative modeling but a structuring input without which context-accurate
load profiles cannot be built. The transferable contribution is the
extraction-and-translation procedure itself, applicable to comparable
zero-telemetry contexts across the Global South.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:sample1}

The global energy landscape is currently undergoing a profound transformation, driven by international commitments to climate change mitigation, sustainable development, and the eradication of severe energy poverty. Achieving universal access to clean, reliable, and affordable modern electricity remains one of the defining challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the 2025 Tracking SDG 7 Report, while global electricity access has reached 92\%, approximately 666 million people remain without power, and current trends indicate that 645 million will still lack access by 2030. Furthermore, the International Energy Agency (IEA) highlights that this deficit is heavily concentrated geographically and demographically: over 80\% of the global population lacking electricity resides in Sub-Saharan Africa, and 84\% live in remote, rural communities.
Within this shifting macro-scenario, energy use around the world keeps evolving in time as a product of a broad range of factors that involve external conditions, technological advancements, and personal decisions. Naturally, this evolution is heterogeneous and highly dependent on contexts. As a result, the energy consumption disparities persist, making decentralized renewable energy solutions—such as solar home systems and mini-grids—absolutely critical to bridging the gap for these isolated populations.

Accurate electricity demand estimation is a fundamental requirement for the sustainable planning, operation, and expansion of power infrastructure \cite{adinkrah_artificial_2025, poblete-cazenave_model_2021, guefano_forecast_2021}. It enables the execution of energy efficiency programs and maintains the delicate balance between generation and consumption, a critical necessity given the difficulty of storing electricity at scale \cite{adinkrah_artificial_2025, kavousian_determinants_2013}. This precision becomes especially vital—and unforgiving—in the context of small, decentralized off-grid networks like mini-grids and Solar Home Systems, where forecasting is directly tied to a project's financial viability and technical survival \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023}. When demand is over-estimated, developers install oversized systems that drastically inflate upfront capital and maintenance costs, extending payback periods and risking financial collapse while unnecessarily increasing emissions \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, blodgett_accuracy_2017, herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022, few_electricity_2022}. Conversely, under-estimating demand leads to undersized systems plagued by poor reliability, accelerated battery degradation, and the loss of critical services like healthcare \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, blodgett_accuracy_2017}. Frequent outages erode community trust, force reliance on expensive diesel backups, and ultimately stunt organic economic growth \cite{herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022, few_electricity_2022}.

To avoid these pitfalls, off-grid operators must perfectly align community demand with the physical realities of generation. In solar-powered microgrids, incentivizing daytime consumption when solar generation peaks can significantly reduce the need for massive battery storage, lowering the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) by 25 percent or more \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023}. Achieving this requires a granular breakdown of demand to identify flexible loads—such as water pumping, agricultural milling, or cold storage—that can be shifted to match real-time generation \cite{kavousian_determinants_2013, few_electricity_2022}. Furthermore, it necessitates targeted Demand-Side Management (DSM) strategies and time-of-use tariffs that are intentionally tailored to the micro-level financial behaviors of distinct customer segments, ensuring that newly stimulated productive uses complement rather than overwhelm the system's evening peaks \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, lorenzoni_classification_2020}.

Traditionally, efforts to capture and simulate this household energy consumption have relied heavily on a structural and economic lens, where income, socio-demographic makeup, and physical dwelling attributes serve as the primary drivers of demand \cite{pachauri_analysis_2004, Kowsari2011, poblete-cazenave_model_2021, richmond_drivers_2020}. While budget constraints, demographic footprints, and stark infrastructural divides dictate basic access and economies of scale \cite{jiang_rural_2020, kavousian_determinants_2013, zhao_survey_2019, azam_factors_2015, dominguez_estimating_2021}, reducing energy consumption to a purely technical or economic process critically misunderstands how human beings live. Extracting the level of granular, flexible demand required for off-grid survival is impossible if planners only measure what and how much is being consumed; understanding the "why" behind energy consumption is absolutely critical. As energy literature increasingly recognizes, asking "What is energy for?" is the missing link in historical modeling efforts, which have traditionally analyzed abstract energy rather than human behavior \cite{Kowsari2011, shove_what_2014}. People do not consume raw electricity for its own sake; they consume "energy services" to accomplish deeply embedded social practices and everyday routines \cite{shove_what_2014, day_conceptualising_2016}.

When this philosophical "why" is revealed, it uncovers the cultural, moral, and social values that strictly dictate consumer choices, often overriding purely technical or economic logic \cite{Kowsari2011}. For instance, households may reject efficient electric stoves because traditional meals cooked over open flames are culturally perceived as superior in taste and appropriateness \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020}. Similarly, the acquisition of specific appliances often serves to claim modernity, differentiate social status, and strengthen community ties rather than simply satisfy a utility need \cite{tesfamichael_eager_2020}, while the seemingly arduous task of gathering traditional biomass often functions as a crucial space for social bonding among women \cite{louw_determinants_2008}. These practices are further differentiated by religion and ethnicity, which historically situate specific marginalized groups to rely on certain fuels \cite{richmond_drivers_2020}. Ultimately, what top-down observers might classify as "energy poverty" often reflects a localized, traditional lifestyle, highlighting that energy behavior is a highly context-specific cultural construct \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020}. When planners treat rural users as purely rational economic actors and ignore these cultural functions, interventions inevitably fail; a new technology cannot replace a traditional one if it cannot perform the specific cultural "work" the household requires \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020, shove_what_2014}.

Despite the deeply human reality of energy use, attempts to mathematically model and estimate energy demand face severe methodological hurdles that systematically minimize this human component. Accurate micro-level data is notoriously scarce, forcing modelers to rely on energy-use surveys that are highly vulnerable to self-reporting errors and "social-desirability bias" \cite{Kowsari2011, blodgett_accuracy_2017, m_heinrich_energy_2022, tesfamichael_eager_2020}. While the integration of physical sensors in recent research has successfully exposed the massive biases in self-reported data, telemetry alone cannot explain the complex behavioral dynamics driving those numbers; physical measurements must act as a complement to surveys, not a replacement \cite{rom_using_2020}. Bereft of accurate behavioral data, energy simulation models frequently oversimplify users, treating them purely as rational economic actors seeking to maximize utility \cite{riva_long-term_2018}. This blind spot generates a significant "energy performance gap" between simulated predictions and actual real-world usage \cite{medojevic_integrated_2021}.

This discrepancy is further exacerbated by the contextual misapplication of global models developed in industrialized nations. These models frequently assume a linear progression up an "energy ladder" based on income and uniform power availability, failing entirely to account for Global South realities like informal economies, suppressed demand, and complex fuel stacking—where exogenous factors like unreliable grids compel households to maintain portfolios of traditional and modern fuels simultaneously \cite{stevanato_data-driven_2025, liu_impact_2022, Muhumuza2018}. While top-down macro models obscure daily load fluctuations, bottom-up stochastic models—though mathematically sophisticated—struggle immensely with defining coincidence factors, often either grossly overestimating peak demand by assuming simultaneous appliance use or flattening the curve by spreading loads too evenly \cite{kuster_electrical_2017, herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022}.

Consequently, standard energy demand models heavily rely on top-down, generalized assumptions that fail to capture the complex, highly localized realities of rural and indigenous communities. Because these conventional models ignore the strict boundaries imposed by socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, and material constraints (such as rigid agricultural routines, specific gender roles, and the physical limits of micro-storage), they generate inaccurate load profiles that do not reflect actual human behavior. This systemic inability to account for the socio-technical context leads directly to the inappropriately sized off-grid systems, unmet community needs, and the widespread failure of rural electrification initiatives discussed previously. Ultimately, a critical methodological gap remains: conventional planning tools lack a systematic way to operationalize local cultural realities into the hard quantitative parameters required for accurate, inclusive electrification planning.

\subsection{Study Objectives and Paper Structure}
\label{sec:objectives}

To directly address this gap, this paper aims to investigate how socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, energy practices, and material factors shape energy behavior in rural communities. By exploring these dynamics through a detailed case study in the Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory of Raqaypampa, Bolivia, the study seeks to generate insights that can enhance the modeling of energy demand, ultimately contributing to the development of sustainable, context-sensitive energy solutions.  

The central research question guiding this work is: \textit{How do socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, and material realities shape energy behavior in an indigenous territory, and how can these insights be operationalized to support bottom-up energy demand modeling for inclusive electrification planning?}

To answer this question, this study introduces a novel mixed-methods pipeline that operationalizes indigenous Quechua knowledge into hard algorithmic boundary conditions within a demand simulation model, effectively replacing standard generalized assumptions with localized empirical reality.  

The key contributions of this paper are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contextualized empirical insights: This paper offers a detailed empirical case study of energy behavior in an isolated Indigenous community, uncovering the socio-cultural, economic, and material factors that shape daily energy practices. While rooted in the specific context of rural Bolivia, the findings offer highly transferable insights for similar underserved, off-grid settings across the Global South. 
    \item Innovative use of the Energy Cultures framework: By applying an extended mixed-methods approach within the Energy Cultures framework, the study advances a holistic analytical lens to capture the complex interplay between cultural norms, daily practices, and material realities in rural energy use—a methodological innovation rarely applied in the context of quantitative energy modeling.  
    \item Bridging data gaps through fieldwork and modeling integration: The paper contributes to the discourse on effective data collection methodologies in data-scarce environments. By triangulating quantitative and qualitative self-reported energy practices with actual measured electricity consumption, the study audits the cognitive "Say-Do" gap, bridging demand data availability issues and improving the accuracy of energy behavior analyses in remote communities.  
    \item Modeling contribution (Integrating socio-cultural dimensions into demand simulation): The study operationalizes qualitative and behavioral insights into the a bottom-up demand simulation model, embedding socio-cultural dimensions directly into demand simulations. Through these empirical insights, it is demonstrated how user practices, priorities, and constraints reshape load profiles and challenge dominant modeling assumptions, offering a more grounded foundation for sufficiency-based electrification planning.
\end{itemize}
 
\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{The energy cultures framework} 

The Energy Cultures Framework (ECF) is an integrative, multidisciplinary heuristic originally developed by Stephenson et al. (2010, 2015) to explore the complex factors driving energy consumption and to identify opportunities for behavioral change \cite{stephenson_energy_2010, stephenson_energy_2015}. Rooted in systems thinking, practice theory, and structuration theory, the framework draws significantly on Bourdieu’s sociological concept of "habitus"—the persistent patterns of thought, perception, and action that emerge in response to an individual's objective conditions \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. By treating energy behavior not just as an economic or technological choice but as a cultural phenomenon, the ECF bridges the gap between individual-focused research and studies on wider social and technological structures \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}.

The foundational premise of the ECF is that consumer energy behavior is fundamentally shaped by the dynamic interactions between three core components: Norms, material culture and practices. Norms encompasses the shared beliefs, expectations, and aspirations people hold regarding their energy practices and material assets \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. Some adaptations of the framework explicitly expand this element to include "attitudes, perceptions \& social norms" to highlight both socially governed rules and individual agency \cite{rau_changing_2020}. Norms can act as a profound driver for change, such as when environmental concern motivates new technological uptake, or as a barrier when traditional views limit the adoption of new practices \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}. Material Culture refers to the physical artifacts, technologies, building structures, and infrastructures involved in energy use \cite{klaniecki_applying_2020, stephenson_energy_2015}. Examples range from home insulation and heating devices to electric vehicles and solar photovoltaic panels. Some researchers prefer the term "material conditions" to maintain a clear conceptual boundary and avoid implying a nested "culture within a culture" \cite{rau_changing_2020}.
Practices are the actual activities that involve energy. Practices range from frequent, routinized, everyday habits (like adjusting a thermostat or running a washing machine) to infrequent actions (like choosing to purchase a new appliance) \cite{klaniecki_applying_2020, stephenson_energy_2015}.


A crucial aspect of the ECF is the high interactivity among these three core elements. Cognitive norms heavily influence the choice of technologies (material culture) and the actions undertaken (practices); material culture shapes both cognitive norms and the range of possible practices; and practices determine how technologies are used while simultaneously shaping beliefs and understandings \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. When norms, practices, and material culture align and become mutually reinforcing, behavior stabilizes and becomes entrenched, creating a strong resistance to change—a state akin to self-replicating "habitus" . Conversely, the potential for behavioral change or a "step-change" in energy efficiency arises when one of these components shifts or becomes misaligned,. For example, the acquisition of a new energy-efficient technology (a change in material culture) can force a shift in practices and eventually alter norms \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}.

The core interacting system of norms, practices, and material culture is embedded within a broader contextual environment. This outer ring of the framework consists of wider systemic influences such as demographics, education, household income, available technologies, building regulations, policy frameworks, and energy pricing structures . These external factors are not exogenous; rather, they exert a profound influence on the energy culture (e.g., energy prices affecting practices, or regulations altering available material culture) and, in turn, are influenced by shifting cultural norms \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}.

The ECF is highly adaptive and multi-scalar. While frequently used to understand individual or household energy transitions (such as residential retrofitting or the adoption of solar home systems) \cite{rau_changing_2020, khan_impacts_2020} it is equally applicable to businesses undergoing energy eco-innovations,local community transitions, and even the macro-level analysis of national decarbonization pathways \cite{stephenson_energy_2021}. 

Furthermore, aggregate analyses often mask the heterogeneity of energy users. The ECF is specifically designed to characterize this variability by identifying distinct clusters of "energy cultures"—segments of a population that share similar patterns of interacting norms, practices, and material cultures \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. By recognizing the heterogeneity of energy consumers, the ECF serves as a powerful diagnostic tool. It allows researchers to cluster populations into distinct "energy cultures" or "behavioral archetypes" based on shared patterns of norms, practices, and material assets \cite{m_heinrich_energy_2022}. For policymakers, this means moving away from a generic, one-size-fits-all approach to behavioral intervention. Instead, by identifying the specific systemic barriers holding a distinct cultural cluster back, policies, marketing, and incentives can be accurately targeted to successfully drive sustainable energy transitions \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}.

\subsection{De-centering the Urban Baseline in Energy Demand Modeling}
The foundational assumptions of standard energy simulation tools are frequently rooted in a Western, urban-centric paradigm of development. These frameworks typically rely on the "energy ladder" model, which theorizes that households act as utility-maximizing consumers, moving in a linear progression away from traditional biomass fuels toward more expensive, modern alternatives as their socio-economic status increases . However, empirical literature demonstrates that this linear model is a theoretical oversimplification \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013}. Instead, households predominantly engage in "energy stacking," utilizing a portfolio of multiple fuels simultaneously \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013, masera}. While energy stacking functions partly as a livelihood strategy to cope with irregular incomes and unreliable supply chains, research shows that fuel choices are also heavily dictated by deep-rooted cultural habits. Traditional practices and taste preferences—such as specific cooking techniques—frequently lead households to retain traditional fuels even when commercial alternatives are affordable . These complexities illustrate that energy choices are not purely economic or efficiency-driven, but are the result of an interactive web of social, cultural, and environmental factors \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013, masera}.

Theoretical critiques of this universalist ontology—which often views nature purely as a physical resource to be exploited for continuous economic expansion—argue that it fundamentally misinterprets the realities of Indigenous and traditional rural communities \cite{shokrgozar_fine_2025}. To move beyond linear paradigms of consumption, scholars increasingly draw upon non-Western epistemologies, such as the South American concept of Buen Vivir (Living Well) \cite{gudynas_buen_2011}. As a theoretical framework, Buen Vivir systematically dismantles the modern dualism between society and nature, rejecting the myth of unidirectional linear progress. Instead, it promotes a relational understanding where human well-being is intrinsically tied to the community and the non-human environment. Under this paradigm, energy is not conceptualized merely as a commodity for individual accumulation. Consequently, what a top-down economic model might diagnose as "energy poverty" or "suppressed demand" is instead understood within this framework as a community practicing intentional sufficiency and cultural preservation.

To accurately conceptualize these realities, contemporary socio-technical literature proposes a "cosmologies of energy" approach \cite{mazzone_indigenous_2022}. Through this theoretical lens, Indigenous epistemologies do not view energy merely as fuel to perform mechanical work; rather, energy creation and usage are understood as being tied to profound cultural narratives, social reciprocity, and traditional knowledge systems. When Western energy models ignore these deep ontological differences, they inherently fail to capture the behavioral drivers of demand, risking the imposition of inappropriate technical fixes that lead to technological rejection. Therefore, advancing energy demand modeling requires frameworks that decenter the urban consumer baseline, actively incorporating Indigenous epistemologies to ensure that simulations accurately reflect local cultural realities rather than imposing generalized Western assumptions.

\subsection{Conceptualizing "Virtual Appliances"}
To successfully integrate the qualitative rules of the Energy Cultures Framework and the nuanced realities of localized epistemologies into the rigid mathematical boundaries of a stochastic simulation engine, a fundamental conceptual shift is required. Traditional engineering demand models standardize physical hardware; they utilize generalized probability matrices for generic devices (e.g., assuming a standard probability distribution for a "3W LED bulb" across a population). This approach treats the hardware as a single entity with uniform usage probabilities spread broadly across a day. However, grounded in Social Practice Theory—a foundational pillar of the Energy Cultures Framework—this study posits that people do not consume energy or operate hardware in the abstract; they perform social practices \cite{shove_what_2014}.  

To operationalize this reality within a bottom-up engines, this research introduces the methodological concept of the "Virtual Appliance". While the term "virtual appliance" is frequently utilized in computer science to describe digital twins of smart devices, or in electrical engineering to describe the aggregation of multiple physical loads for grid forecasting, this study reappropriates and entirely redefines the concept for socio-technical modeling. Here, a Virtual Appliance is not conceptualized as a physical piece of plastic and wire, but rather as a mathematically bounded human routine. Instead of modeling a singular physical device, this approach programmatically disaggregates physical hardware into multiple, behavior-specific sociological entities. For example, a single physical LED bulb is theoretically split into distinct Virtual Appliances, such as a "Morning Agricultural Prep Light" versus an "Evening Domestic Cooking Light".  

By standardizing the human practice rather than the physical item, the simulation aligns perfectly with actual behavioral rigidity. Each Virtual Appliance is assigned an isolated, empirically derived hourly probability matrix and a non-negotiable time window dictated strictly by the cultural norms and material constraints of that specific household profile. This theoretical synthesis ensures that the stochastic model respects both human cultural intent and the physical realities of the infrastructure without relying on generalized engineering assumptions, successfully bridging the gap between sociological theory and engineering algorithms.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology} 

According to \cite{zou_mixed_2018}, studies addressing building occupant energy behavior use mainly quantitative methods. However, while these methods hold a solid background, it has been demonstrated that qualitative approaches are needed to understand better social phenomena involving people energy behavior. Furthermore, the use of sensors in research has demonstrated biases in self-reported energy use, indicating that while physical measurements are critical, they are not enough by themselves to understand behavior dynamics and must be used as a complement to surveys \cite{rom_using_2020}.

To address this gap, several studies explored the use of mixed-methods in the context of energy, from technology adoption \cite{university_of_augsburg_adoption_2019, palm_peer_2017} to understand energy practices \cite{rosenberg_evidence_2019, guerra-santin_mixed_2016, wolff_tackling_2017}. Mixed methods involves the use of qualitative and quantitative data, from rigorous collection procedures and analysis, to creative forms of integrating them \cite{creswell_research_nodate}. 

A pragmatic worldview is adopted for this work, under the method of a Case Study. The framework of the Energy Cultures proposed by Stephenson et al. \cite{stephenson_energy_2010} is used. Due to the nature of the dimensions of this framework previously explained, the choice of a mixed methods research design is strongly justified. \subsection{Study Area: An Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory}
\label{sec:case_study}

The study area is located within the Indigenous Originary Peasant Autonomous Territory (TIOC) of Raqaypampa in Bolivia, inhabited by Quechua communities. Officially recognized as the first TIOC in the country on July 12, 2016, Raqaypampa marked a milestone in indigenous self-determination. Its Autonomy Statute was approved by referendum in November 2016, with over 91\% voter support, followed by the first elections of autonomous authorities in June 2017. Grounded in ancestral values and the principle of Sumaq Kawsay (Living Well), the territory is governed through holistic, culturally rooted practices aimed at managing the land in harmony with Pachamama (Mother Earth). Today, Raqaypampa serves as a national reference for community-led governance. The territory encompasses 43 communities across approximately 556 square kilometers, with elevations ranging from 1,600 to 3,300 meters above sea level (See Figure \ref{fig:raq_map}) \cite{oep_estatuto_2016, plan de gestion territorial}. 

Between 2001 and 2012, the territory experienced a significant population decline, dropping from approximately 10,230 to 7,340 inhabitants. This decrease was largely attributed to permanent migration driven by economic hardship and the absence of basic services. However, according to the most recent national census conducted in 2024, the population has increased slightly, reaching 8,007 inhabitants. 

Household sizes in the targeted communities range from 1 to 12 members (66 households located in Zone A and 32 in Zone B). Families are predominantly organized as nuclear, extended, or large extended households, with 75\% led by male heads of household. Dwellings are typically constructed from mud bricks with earthen floors and roofs of clay tile, corrugated metal, or straw. Living spaces are generally shared, with separate auxiliary structures frequently built for cooking with firewood and storing food (known locally as trofas). An example of this structural distribution is shown in Figure \ref{fig:household}.

The local economy relies heavily on rain-fed, subsistence agriculture and livestock farming, with occasional surplus sold in local markets. Livelihoods remain highly precarious; at least 60\% of households report earning less than USD 35 per month or having no direct monetary income. The communities lack access to potable water infrastructure, relying instead on natural springs and rivers that are highly vulnerable to drought. Diets are largely based on locally grown maize, potatoes, and vegetables, supplemented by rice.

Because the communities lack irrigation, survival and economic stability are strictly tethered to the rainy season. This establishes a rigid annual baseline calendar: soil preparation occurs in August–September, sowing in October–November, crop maintenance throughout the rains (October–March), and harvesting in May–June. This foundational seasonality dictates the economic, physical, and temporal constraints under which the implemented Solar Home Systems operate.

\subsection{Multi-Stream Data Collection}
\label{sec:data_collection_analysis} 

To capture both the physical realities of the SHS and the behavioral dynamics of the users, this study deploys a convergent mixed-methods design comprising three parallel streams of data collection (summarized in \ref{tab:data_collection}). Each stream was deliberately designed to capture specific dimensions of the Energy Cultures framework  and generate distinct data artifacts for the final modeling phase:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stream A: Qualitative Tracking (Norms \& Aspirations)}. 
    To understand the cultural "why" behind the physical data, a qualitative campaign was executed. This included 50 semi-structured interviews with household heads and key informants, alongside 2 focus group discussions to explore daily habits, gender roles, energy-sharing norms, and seasonal traditions.
    \item \textbf{Stream B: Socioeconomic and Practices Surveys (External Factors \& Self-Reported Practices)}. 
    To capture the demographic boundaries and perceived behaviors of the community, structured quantitative surveys were deployed. A baseline socioeconomic survey was conducted with 100 household heads at the time of SHS deployment (February 2023) to capture demographics, livelihoods, and structural constraints. Subsequently, a follow-up structured survey was conducted with 53 household members to systematically map self-reported energy practices.
    \item \textbf{Stream C: Empirical Load Data (Material Culture \& Actual Practices)}. 
    To capture the objective physical reality of energy consumption, continuous data loggers were installed in a subset of 14 SHS households. Operating from April 2023 to March 2026, these sensors recorded real-time, high-resolution electricity telemetry. The primary data artifacts extracted from this quantitative stream were the true baseline wattages, hardware voltage, and the precise empirical timing of appliance use, which served as the absolute physical ground-truth for validation.
\end{itemize}

The data from these three streams were processed independently before convergence (as visualized in \ref{fig:methods}). Quantitative survey data (Stream B) were analyzed using descriptive and correlational statistical techniques via Python to establish the socioeconomic profiles. 

Qualitative transcripts and field notes (Stream A) were systematically coded using the open-source software QualCoder. The coding process followed a hybrid deductive-inductive approach, utilizing the three core dimensions of the Energy Cultures framework (Material Culture, Norms, and Practices) to generate a foundational codebook, while allowing context-specific sub-codes to emerge organically. The thematic data was processed through a three-step analytical pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identification of Temporal Anchors: Initial analysis utilized code frequency reports strictly within the Practices category to identify the core energy events defining the physical load profile. High-frequency codes are isolated as the primary temporal anchors of the community's energy demand.
    \item Relational Matrix Analysis: Recognizing that energy practices do not occur in a vacuum, relationship-based mapping was employed. Using matrix tools, co-occurrence queries were executed to overlap the temporal anchors with specific demographic vectors and cultural norms. This step identified the underlying socio-cultural drivers of energy use, mapping specific household profiles against specific peak demand events to determine who was driving the demand and why.
    \item Logic Formulation: The intersections identified in the relational matrix were systematically translated into narrative logic rules. Analytical memos were generated using a standardized syntactical formula mapping specific norms and demographics to distinct practices under defined material constraints: 
    
\[[Norm/Demographic] drives [Practice] under [Material Conditions]\]

\end{enumerate}

Through this three-step pipeline, the thematic synthesis successfully extracted a discrete set of rigid social rules governing daily energy use. These rules served as the empirical, logical foundation for the subsequent mathematical parameterization in the demand modeling phase.

Empirical load data collected from the physical sensor network comprise high-resolution, time-series energy demand. Initially, the raw datasets undergo a rigorous cleaning phase to ensure data integrity and standardize temporal intervals. Following this preparatory step, a quantitative analysis of the measured load curves is performed. This computational process involves the direct extraction of key energy use parameters, alongside the calculation of diverse statistical indicators designed to systematically evaluate the magnitude, variability, reliability, and structural shape of the physical electricity demand.

\subsection{Data integration and modeling}
The mixed-methods approach culminates in the integration of the three independent data streams to create a context-specific demand model. This convergence occurs in three distinct phases: energy behavior profiles classification, virtual appliance parametrization and demand simulation.


\subsubsection{Energy Behavior Profile classification}
\label{sec:ebp_classification}

Households are classified into Energy Behavior Profiles (EBPs) through a procedure that
integrates the qualitative socio-cultural rules (Stream~A) with demographic survey variables
(Stream~B) in four stages: qualitative typology emergence, deterministic operationalization,
data-completeness handling, and validation.

\textbf{Qualitative typology emergence.} During thematic coding, specific subsets of the
extracted socio-technical rules were observed to co-occur consistently within the same types of
households, suggesting a latent typology in which distinct rule-bundles govern distinct segments
of the population rather than applying uniformly across it. These emergent clusters were first
observed during the coding process and subsequently confirmed through the relational matrix
analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:data_collection_analysis}, where co-occurrence queries
verified that the identified rules systematically appeared together for households sharing
similar characteristics. This qualitative step, not survey data, is the origin of the resulting
profiles.

\textbf{Deterministic operationalization.} Because the socio-technical rules are formulated
around specific actors and social roles, they do not merely correlate with demographic
categories --- they explicitly name them. The variables that operationalize each profile are
therefore not selected through statistical screening but are read directly from the qualitative
rule formulations themselves: the actors named by each rule-bundle are matched to their
corresponding survey fields, producing a small set of structural proxies. These proxies extend
the qualitatively-derived typology beyond the subset of households for which qualitative material
exists, to the full surveyed population, by operationalizing it as a fixed, priority-ordered
decision tree, evaluated in strict order and stopping at the first match: occupational or
structural overrides are checked first, isolating households whose routine is defined by an
occupation or living arrangement that dominates any other demographic signal; family-structure
and life-stage variables are then applied in sequence to resolve the remainder, with each
subsequent variable narrowing ambiguity left by the one before it. This tree is applied to the
full population for which every required field is available across the relevant survey wave(s),
a scope constraint that is a deliberate design decision rather than an incidental sample loss.
The full decision logic, variable identities, and resulting counts are reported alongside the
profiles in Section~\ref{sec:ebp_results}.

\textbf{Data-completeness handling.} Missing required fields are resolved via a sensitivity check
rather than a silent default: every plausible value for each missing field is substituted and the
rule re-run; if all combinations agree, that profile is assigned, and if not, the household is
left explicitly unclassified rather than resolved by assumption.

\textbf{Correction and validation.} Two distinct correction mechanisms may be applied downstream
of the decision tree, and are kept categorically separate because they draw on different evidence
and carry different justificatory weight. First, where qualitative fieldwork indicates a specific
coded field --- never the profile label itself --- was miscoded for a specific household, the
field is corrected at the source with a documented justification, and the unchanged decision tree
is rerun; any change in profile assignment follows mechanically from the corrected field. Second,
structural conditions that are not fully observable through any single fixed survey variable ---
such as a household's energy-consuming presence being split across two physical residences ---
are treated separately: where qualitative sources provide corroborated evidence, from more than
one independent account, of such a condition that the survey-derived classification does not
reflect, the profile assignment is overridden directly to the classification appropriate to that
condition. Both correction types, together with their evidentiary basis and effect on final
counts, are logged in Supplementary Material S2.

The finished classification is subsequently validated, not re-derived, in two independent ways.
Qualitatively, a concordance check screens interview and memo text for each classified household
against thematic markers associated with its assigned profile, flagging disagreement for manual
review; this step never edits the classification directly. Quantitatively, correlation analysis of
each profile's demographic and behavioral survey fields is used to confirm discriminant validity
--- that classified groups are behaviorally distinct along the dimensions their governing rules
predict --- reported alongside the profiles in Section~\ref{sec:ebp_results}.

Because the classification criteria are entirely survey-derived, every household within scope is
assigned to exactly one profile. However, only the interviewed subset contributes the qualitative
material used in the parametrization that follows
(Section~\ref{sec:va_parametrization}). Population counts per profile, split by survey-derived
and interviewed subpopulations, are reported in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsubsection{Virtual Appliance parametrization}
\label{sec:va_parametrization}
Once households are classified, the qualitative rules defining each EBP establish strict temporal boundaries on energy-related practices. Rather than treating a household's energy use as an undifferentiated set of generic appliances,
practice timing is first reconstructed qualitatively as an \textit{Anthropological Window}: a
culturally bound segment of the household's typical day (e.g., the evening domestic block)
built from interview narrative, describing what a household is generally doing rather than any
single appliance's operating parameters. Each Virtual Appliance's specific numeric window is then
derived by triangulating this Anthropological Window against independent survey time-use fields
and hard physical anchors (sunrise/sunset). Because the Anthropological Window and the resulting
VA window are derived from partially independent evidence --- narrative account versus recorded
survey field --- the fit between them is itself checkable, and reported as a triangulation
consistency finding where the underlying data permits.

This translation from qualitative account to quantitative parameter follows a systematic protocol, so that no value is left without a declared, auditable basis. Every parameter traces to one of five derivation sources (Table~\ref{tab:derivation_sources}): two are direct technical inputs, one is the triangulated Anthropological Window described above, and two are \textbf{crosswalks} -- classification steps that sort respondent language into quantitative bins.

A single qualitative classification of how rigid versus trigger-driven a practice is -- anchored to an external constraint (Strict), shifting with daily circumstance but describable (Flexible), or unanchored and unpredictable from the respondent's own account (Chaos) -- propagates into three separate model parameters, directly for two of them and, via a second-order fraction-of-\texttt{func\_time} rule, for the third (\texttt{func\_cycle}). One qualitative judgment therefore does the work that three independently justified quantitative estimates would otherwise require. This is the clearest instantiation of the paper's central claim: qualitative understanding does not merely supplement quantitative modeling, it structures it.

Each parameter value is intended to reflect observed behavioral patterns as reported by respondents, rather than to constitute an independently validated ground truth; the degree to which these values approximate measured telemetry is reported as a validation finding in Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}. 

Because the \texttt{ebp\_profile} classification (Section~\ref{sec:ebp_classification}) is itself survey-derived, every household assigned to a profile has survey data, but only a subset was also interviewed. Each profile therefore carries two population counts: \textbf{N\_survey}, all households classified into the profile, and \textbf{N\_interview}, the subset with transcript material -- the sole source of Windows, rigidity classification, and \texttt{occasional\_use} values. All qualitative parameters are derived from N\_interview and applied uniformly across N\_survey at simulation time; this generalization step is stated explicitly for each profile in the corresponding truth file.

The complete crosswalk tables, worked parameter derivations with anchor quotes, and the protocol's revision history are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsubsection{Simulation and validation strategy}
\label{sec:methods_sim}

To isolate and quantify the value of integrating qualitative data into quantitative energy demand modeling, a comparative A/B validation strategy is designed. The objective of this phase is to evaluate whether socio-technical parameters can successfully capture detailed behavior when modeling energy consumption.

Two distinct simulation models are built with RAMP, each representing a fundamentally different methodological approach for parametrization. 

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model A (Standard baseline)}: assumes that, because all households share identical Solar Home System hardware, they can be represented as a single type. A single representative profile is built from community-wide survey averages: appliance inventory follows the standard SHS accessory set; time windows span the average wake-up-to-departure and sunset-to-sleep periods (two continuous blocks); \texttt{func\_time} is the survey-wide mean duration per activity; \texttt{func\_cycle} equals \texttt{func\_time}, so no intra-window cycling occurs; and \texttt{occasional\_use} is a single static year-round average. Requiring only Stream B data and a hardware audit, this parametrization is fast and low-cost.
\item \textbf{Model B (Socio-Technical Approach)}: parametrizes the simulation using the Virtual Appliances derived in Section~\ref{sec:va_parametrization}, with time windows, cycle structure, and use probability differentiated by the rigidity and routines of each Energy Behavior Profile (EBP) rather than pooled into a single community average. It requires the qualitative derivation protocol in addition to Stream B data.
\end{itemize}

To evaluate performance, the simulated load curves from both Model A and Model B are computationally compared against the high-resolution data captured by the physical dataloggers. Because decentralized household load curves are highly volatile and driven by human activities, the analysis relies on a Dual-Tier Validation Strategy. This approach captures not only point-by-point magnitude and temporal alignment, but also the macro-level structural variability of human routines.

Tier 1 (Time-Series Validation) evaluates the daily chronological alignment of the models. For this foundational engineering benchmark, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) is utilized to evaluate the exact magnitude of deviation between synthetic and measured time-series data, a widely established approach for assessing simulated behavioral variations \cite{kohler_similarity_2022, nti_electricity_2020, adeoye_modelling_2019, widen_constructing_2009}. However, because RMSE heavily penalizes minor temporal shifts in human routines, it is paired with the Longest Common Subsequence (LCSS). LCSS is employed to measure exact shape matching and chronological sequence alignment, effectively accommodating human behavioral elasticity without artificially inflating the error rate \cite{zhang_electricity_2022, kohler_similarity_2022}. Furthermore, to specifically evaluate the model’s ability to capture extreme demand events, the Mean Percentage Deviation Against Daily Average (MPDADA) is utilized to mathematically isolate and assess peak demand predictions \cite{ihsane_new_2018}.

Tier 2 (Structural Profiling) ensures the simulation escapes the mathematical "flaw of averages" by validating the emergent, socio-technical properties of the dataset. These specific temporal and magnitude indicators serve as macro-level validation benchmarks for the simulated output. Relative Mean Power, calculated across distinct daily time blocks, is employed to accurately classify active periods driven by localized social routines. This is complemented by the Modal Daily Peak Load Time, which directly validates the habitual occurrence of these maximum demand periods. To capture the non-active footprint of the household, the Minimum Value, or Base Load, is extracted to represent the lowest continuous power demand occurring in the dataset \cite{kohler_similarity_2022, balasubramanian_characterising_2021, li_seasonal_2018}. 

Finally, Tier 2 Validation also extends to system resilience and behavioral irregularity. To validate that the simulation successfully replicates households that engage in highly irregular behaviors—such as extended seasonal absences or informal infrastructure modifications—the Mean Relative Standard Deviation (MRSD) is employed as a variability indicator to measure the day-to-day structural chaos and irregularity of consumer routines.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results are presented in three stages, moving from qualitative pattern to quantitative validation. Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture} identifies the socio-technical rules governing household energy behavior and the demographic variables that statistically distinguish them, converging on four Energy Behavior Profiles (EBPs). Section~\ref{sec:virtual-appliance} translates these qualitative rules into the Virtual Appliance parameters used to drive the RAMP simulation, showing how identical physical hardware is disaggregated into behaviorally distinct loads across profiles. Section~\ref{sec:validation} then evaluates whether this socio-technical parametrization (Model B) improves on a conventional,
survey-only baseline (Model A) against empirical data, across both aggregate accuracy and structural load-shape metrics.

\subsection{The Energy Culture of Raqaypampa}

The integration of qualitative tracking (Stream A) and structured household surveys (Stream B) reveals that electricity demand in Raqaypampa is not randomly distributed, nor is it driven purely by appliance ownership. Instead, energy behavior is strictly governed by an underlying matrix of cultural norms, demographic shifts, and material constraints --- and this governance is legible, almost word-for-word, in how households describe their own routines.

Through relationship-based thematic analysis of code co occurrences, 17 distinct socio-technical rules were extracted. These rules act as the governing logic for daily energy consumption and are organized into four operational themes.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Daily Baselines (Predictable Anchors).} Daily load curves are heavily anchored by rigid cultural priorities. The \textit{Educational Anchor} (Rule 1) dictates that evening lighting windows (18:00--22:00) are fiercely protected for children's homework --- as one respondent put it plainly when asked why the household first adopted the system: ``we needed lighting to study --- before we used candles... the greatest benefit is for studying and doing homework'' [P90: ``necesitábamos iluminación, para estudiar usábamos velas... el mayor beneficio es para estudiar y para hacer los deberes'']. The \textit{Agricultural Dictate} (Rule 2) forces extreme early-morning routines to maximize daylight hours in the fields --- respondents describe rising and lighting the house as early as 04:00--05:00 before departing for the day [P63: ``Me levanto a las 4 o 5 de la mañana... hago alumbrar desde las 6 de la tarde hasta las 10 de la noche'']. Evening energy use is further gendered by the \textit{Gendered Anchor of Domestic Operations} (Rule 3), where adult women managing food preparation --- typically two to three cooking events per day over an open wood fire (\textit{fogón}) --- define the temporal start of peak evening demand. The \textit{Connectivity Lifeline} (Rule 4) reveals that for elderly households whose children have migrated permanently to the Chapare or nearby cities, the cell phone is treated as an emotional necessity rather than a discretionary device: respondents describe living permanently at the homestead while children live elsewhere [R: ``Mis hijos están en el Chapare. Mis nietos viven aquí conmigo''], and stress how essential a working charge is precisely because of that distance [R: ``es tan necesario para comunicarnos cuando uno está a distancias separadas'']. Complementing this, the \textit{Digital Delegation} (Rule 5) reveals that even where an elder or adult nominally owns the household's phone, day-to-day operation is routinely delegated to the youngest capable household member --- elders disclaim operational competence outright rather than describing infrequent use: ``my youngest son is the one who uses the phone; I don't use it'' [R: ``Mi hijo menor es el que utiliza el celular; yo no lo utilizo''], and ``I also have two grandchildren; they're the ones who handle phones, not me'' [R: ``Yo también tengo dos nietos; ellos son los que manejan celulares, yo no'']. This delegation persists outside the home as well: ``I only have it here; I don't know how to operate it when I go out'' [R: ``Aquí nomás lo tengo; no sé manejarlo cuando salgo'']. Where Rule 4 explains why a household values a charged phone, Rule 5 explains who actually operates it --- predicting that charging frequency tracks the presence of a youth or school-aged resident rather than the presence of the nominal owner.

\item \textbf{System Constraints and Coping Mechanisms.} Users dynamically alter their behavior based on environmental constraints. Under \textit{Weather-Driven Load Shedding} (Rule 6), families proactively
ration light and shift cell phone charging strictly to peak daylight hours during cloudy weather: ``a sunny day is a good option to charge it. But when it's cloudy, we... ration'' [P: ``el día soleado es buena opción para cargarlo. Pero cuando es nublado, nosotros... (racionamos)'']. The \textit{Safety Baseline} (Rule 7) --- driven not by anticipatory fear but by documented harm from nocturnal darkness --- compels specific demographics to leave at least one LED light illuminated until dawn, significantly altering the daily Depth of Discharge (DoD). One respondent grounds this rule in direct lived experience:
recounting a scorpion sting suffered while sleeping in the dark, she describes how the SHS changed the household's relationship to nighttime safety entirely --- ``once a bug bit me, I think it was a scorpion... now that we have light, there are no longer bugs that bite us... now that there's light, we can see; if there's some bug we can even kill it. Before, in the darkness, you couldn't see... our children sometimes step on something in the dark; that's why this lighting is the best thing'' [P: ``Una vez me picó un bicho, creo que era alacrán... Ahora que hay la luz, ya no hay bichos que nos piquen... Ahora que hay luz, sí vemos; si hay algún bicho lo podemos hasta matar. Antes por la oscuridad no se veía... Nuestros hijos a veces en la oscuridad pisan algo; por eso esta iluminación es lo mejor'']. This account is the rule's clearest evidentiary anchor: the practice is not a diffuse anxiety but a specific,
remembered harm. \textit{Infrastructure Mobility} (Rule 8) reveals that young men frequently dismantle and transport the solar panels to temporary agricultural camps, causing extended localized demand drop-offs --- a practice respondents name with a specific verb, ``trajinar'' (to haul/relocate), and describe as a known behavior that not everyone engages in: ``others know how to `trajinar' it [move it around]... I don't do that myself'' [P?: ``Otros lo saben `trajinar' [trasladar]... yo no lo hago''],
while another household confirms doing exactly this: ``sometimes we haul it out to the hills, to go work'' [P?: ``a veces lo `trajinamos' de vez en cuando a los montes, para ir a trabajar'']. Finally, \textit{Nature as a Threat} (Rule 9) shows that culturally reinforced fear of lightning strikes leads users to avoid system maintenance during the rainy season: ``I heard that when lightning strikes it ruins [the panel]; during the rainy season we don't clean it because the thunder and lightning scare us. I also heard it killed people --- they say lightning struck the panel while people were cleaning it'' [R: ``Escuché que cuando cae rayo lo arruina; en épocas de lluvia no limpiamos porque el trueno y el rayo nos
asusta. Escuché también que mató a gente'']. This single account has real methodological consequence: it is a documented reason to expect elevated soiling losses and disconnection events during the rainy season, distinct from generation losses attributable to cloud cover alone.

\item \textbf{Structural Anomalies).} Traditional modeling assumes static
infrastructure, but the realities of extreme isolation and unmet aspiration breed adaptation. The \textit{Aspiration Gap and Dual-Home Displacement} (Rule 10) reveals that households whose desire for
modern appliances (television, blender) cannot be met by the SHS do not abandon that desire --- they delegate heavy energy use to a second, grid-connected residence while zoning the SHS home strictly for survival needs: ``we do our weaving and other tasks generally at our other house... there we also go to charge our phones... but if it's at the other house where there's ELFEC light, we go there to do that'' [R: ``Sus tareas y algunos trabajos --- como tejer u otras tareas --- los realizamos en nuestra otra casa generalmente... si es en la otra casa donde hay luz de ELFEC, ahí nos vamos para hacer eso'']. This is the clearest explanation for why some households' SHS load curves appear artificially flat: demand has not
disappeared, it has relocated. \textit{Protective Gatekeeping} (Rule 11) shows that fear of damaging a fragile, irreplaceable asset overrides communal sharing norms: ``they do come, but sometimes I think they
might ruin it, and that's why I sometimes don't want to lend it... they told me it gets ruined, so that's why I don't want to lend it; even if they get upset or angry, what can I do? I have to take care of it''
[R: ``Sí vienen, pero a veces pienso que lo pueden arruinar y por eso a veces no quiero prestarlo... Me dijeron que se arruina, entonces por eso no quiero prestarlo; aunque se disgustan o se enojan, ¿pero qué
puedo hacer? Lo tengo que cuidar'']. \textit{Community Override} (Rule 12) demonstrates that routine conservation is temporarily suspended during major community events: ``once the light went out during a wake [\textit{velorio}]. We used the lighting for two full nights and then it went out'' [R: ``Una vez se apagó la luz cuando había velorio. Como dos noches enteras utilizamos el alumbrado y después se apagó'']--- a single account that anchors the model's need for rare, high-impact stochastic spikes rather than a smoothly conservative baseline. Finally, \textit{Hardware Resignation} (Rule 13) captures how minor hardware failures become permanent load reductions rather than repairs, given limited technical literacy and absent supply chains: ``it didn't even last a month; from that very moment it broke'' [R: ``No me duró ni un mes; desde ese momento mismo se arruinó''].

\item \textbf{Productive Labor and Multi-Energy Realities.} The provision of basic lighting does not automatically trigger nighttime economic activity, nor does electrification eliminate other energy sources. The \textit{Productive Lighting Veto} (Rule 14) reveals that adult women actively refuse to weave under LED light --- not from lack of desire, but because the light is functionally inadequate and actively damaging to the material: respondents are explicit that weaving happens by daylight, not by
panel light, ``because you can't see well at night with the solar panel's light'' [P: ``como no se ve bien en la noche con la luz del panel solar, entonces en el día hacemos nuestros trabajos''], and that moths drawn to the light damage the wool and the labor invested in it: ``the moths cause damage --- it's not worth it, since we've also invested time'' [P: ``las polillas perjudican o causan daño; no nos conviene porque invertimos tiempo también'']. The \textit{Thermal Weather Switch} (Rule 15) shows that
weather, not just solar generation, governs cooking fuel choice: households default to gathered firewood because carrying gas cylinders on foot over roadless terrain is prohibitive, but switch to hoarded LPG
gas when firewood is wet: ``we generally use a wood-fired stove to prepare our food. When it rains, we sometimes use a bit of gas cooking --- very rarely, because it's difficult to go buy the gas'' [R: ``Generalmente usamos fogón a leña para preparar nuestra alimentación. Cuando llueve, a veces usamos un poco de cocina con gas; muy poco, porque es difícil ir a comprar el gas'']. The \textit{Gratitude/Scarcity Baseline} (Rule 16) grounds a conservative behavioral default in lived memory of pre-electrification hardship: ``before we only had a \textit{mechero}, which gave off a lot of smoke... we got sick from it, because it's a lot of smoke... now this help from the solar panel I really like'' [R: ``Antes solo teníamos mechero, que desprendía mucho humo... Desde eso nos enfermamos, porque es mucho humo... Ahora esta ayuda del panel solar realmente me gusta'']. Finally, the \textit{Sedentary Anchor} (Rule 17) shows that physical limitations of aging exempt elderly households from the seasonal labor migration that drives Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8) elsewhere in the sample: ``I live here permanently, I don't go
anywhere'' [R: ``Yo vivo aquí permanentemente, no voy a ningún lado''] --- anchoring these households to a continuous, year-round baseline load rather than the stochastic seasonal absences seen in mobile households.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Energy Behavior Profiles (EBP)}
\label{sec:ebp_results}

Before any survey variable was consulted, four profiles emerged from thematic coding of the
interview corpus (Stream A) itself: specific rule-bundles were observed to co-occur consistently
within the same types of households, rather than distributing uniformly across the sample. The
Educational Anchor (Rule 1), Agricultural Dictate (Rule 2), Gendered Anchor of Domestic Operations
(Rule 3), and Digital Delegation (Rule 5) consistently co-occurred among working-age nuclear
households with school-aged children --- motivating the Educational/Agricultural Core. The
Connectivity Lifeline (Rule 4), Safety Baseline (Rule 7), and Sedentary Anchor (Rule 17)
consistently co-occurred among elder-headed households with no co-resident working-age adults or
young children --- motivating the Isolated Elderly. The extended-cooking variant of the Gendered
Anchor (Rule 3), Aspiration Gap and Dual-Home Displacement (Rule 10), and Community Override
(Rule 12) consistently co-occurred among multi-generational households under one roof ---
motivating the Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub. Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8) and Hardware
Resignation (Rule 13) consistently co-occurred among households whose occupation structurally
decoupled them from the settlement's routine --- motivating the System Breakers.

To operationalize this qualitative typology for the full surveyed population --- not only the
interviewed subset --- each profile was mapped to a specific, already-collected structural proxy,
read directly from the actors and material conditions the rules themselves name, rather than
discovered by statistical screening: \texttt{family\_type} (naming the household-structure actors
of Rule 3 and the Structural Anomalies rules, Rules 10 and 12), \texttt{occupation} (naming the
mobile and adaptive actors of Infrastructure Mobility, Rule 8), \texttt{children\_in\_school}
(naming the actors of the Educational Anchor, Rule 1), \texttt{migration}, and
\texttt{portability\_shs} (jointly naming the stability conditions distinguishing a settled
extended household from a mobile one).

These five variables were applied as a fixed, priority-ordered decision tree to the N = 65
households with complete coverage across both survey waves. This scope constraint is a
deliberate design decision, not an incidental sample loss.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Occupation override.} Any household headed by a Student or a Miner routes directly
to \textbf{Profile 4 (The System Breakers)} regardless of family structure, capturing the
occupational actors of Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8) and Hardware Resignation (Rule 13) before
any demographic criterion is considered.
\item \textbf{Solo/couple structure.} Among the remainder, households coded as Unipersonal or
Pareja nuclear route to \textbf{Profile 2 (The Isolated Elderly)}, capturing the actors named by
the Safety Baseline (Rule 7), Connectivity Lifeline (Rule 4), and Sedentary Anchor (Rule 17).
\item \textbf{Educational core, nuclear or stable-extended.} Among the remainder, nuclear
households with children enrolled in school route to \textbf{Profile 1 (The
Educational/Agricultural Core)} --- and so do extended households with enrolled children that
are additionally stable, defined as no temporary migration and the SHS kept at home. This
stability clause keeps a settled, multi-generational household with school-aged children inside
Profile 1 rather than defaulting to Profile 3 purely on family-type code, capturing the
Educational Anchor (Rule 1) and Digital Delegation (Rule 5) actors regardless of household size.
\item \textbf{Extended residual.} Among the remainder, extended-family households route to
\textbf{Profile 3 (The Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub)}, capturing the Gendered Anchor of Domestic
Operations (Rule 3) and Community Override (Rule 12) actors --- households whose structure is
extended but which failed Profile 1's stability clause, typically through active migration or a
portable SHS.
\item \textbf{Residual.} Everyone not captured above falls to Profile 4, alongside the
occupation-override cases from step 1 --- so Profile 4's population combines two distinct
mechanisms: households whose occupation structurally decouples them from the settlement's
routine, and households whose family structure resolves cleanly into neither the stable-extended
nor solo/couple categories.
\end{enumerate}

Applying this tree yields Profile 1 ($n=28$), Profile 2 ($n=11$), Profile 3 ($n=14$), and
Profile 4 ($n=12$). One household required the missing-data sensitivity check described in
\S3.3.1; zero households remained unclassified. Only a subset of each profile's survey-classified
population was also interviewed, and only that subset supplies the qualitative material
underlying the Virtual Appliance parametrization in \S4.2: Profile 1
($n_{\text{interview}}=18$, 64\% coverage), Profile 2 ($n_{\text{interview}}=9$, 82\%), Profile 3
($n_{\text{interview}}=9$, 64\%), and Profile 4 ($n_{\text{interview}}=6$, 50\%). Profile 4's
lower coverage is a specific limitation worth flagging directly: it is also the profile carrying
the dual-residence and structural-absence claims (Rule 10) that most need qualitative grounding,
so its parametrization rests on the thinnest evidentiary base of the four.

Independently of this classification mechanism, correlation analysis of the resulting groups'
quantitative survey data confirms the profiles are behaviorally distinct along exactly the
dimensions their governing rules predict --- evidence of discriminant validity, not the basis on
which households were assigned. Family type strongly shapes daytime phone charging
($\eta = 0.76$ for a secondary device, $\eta = 0.72$ for a primary device), agricultural labor
hours ($\eta = 0.67$), and cooking duration ($\eta = 0.56$), confirming Profile 3's competitive
USB-port and extended-cooking signature. Elderly presence correlates negatively with both
working-age adults ($r = -0.441$, $p < 0.001$) and young children ($r = -0.270$, $p = 0.030$),
and drives a delayed, flatter morning lighting curve ($\eta = 0.517$), confirming Profile 2's
demographic isolation. Presence of school-aged children correlates negatively with wake-up times
($r = -0.323$, $p = 0.019$) and drives early-morning radio ($\eta = 0.491$) and lighting
($\eta = 0.540$) use, plus a positive correlation with total household cell phones
($r = 0.269$, $p = 0.031$), confirming Profile 1's rigid morning anchor and Digital Delegation
dynamic (Rule 5).

In Profile 1, the evening light illustrates the paper's central claim --- that a single qualitative judgment of rigidity does the work of three independently justified quantitative estimates. Interview accounts place this practice firmly anchored to dinner and homework with no reported drift in timing, supporting a \textbf{Strict} rigidity classification \textbf{[RIG-XW]}, which propagates into \texttt{func\_time} = $240 \times (1-0.20) = 192$ minutes \textbf{[WINDOW$\rightarrow$margin]} and \texttt{func\_cycle} = $0.70 \times 192 \approx 130$ minutes \textbf{[RIG-XW$\rightarrow$ func\_time]}, the latter's ratio reduced from the canonical 0.83 to satisfy RAMP's window-jitter engine constraint. No separate evidence is needed for either downstream number once rigidity is classified. Portable charging's \texttt{func\_time} (420 min) combines a directly-evidenced 5-hour daily charging routine with an additional 2 hours attributed to other devices sharing the same port, rather than a single respondent duration.

Profile 2 models lighting as a single functional bulb rather than two independent ones: the second-room fixture is retired, since no household reported running both simultaneously due to the reduced family members of these households. Morning and evening light are Chaos-classified --- less anchored than the working-age profiles' equivalent practices --- while the overnight light, though the least frequent lighting practice in this profile (0.27), is the most rigid: once switched on, it runs as a single unbroken block until dawn.

The secondary light of Profile 3 no longer shadows the main bulb at a flat ratio for every practice: morning light runs one rigidity bin below the main bulb's, reflecting the profile's stated assumption that one light is used for cooking and the other typically is not, while evening and overnight light retain the profile's 0.93 active-second-bulb scaling. This profile carries the highest evening and overnight lighting \texttt{occasional\_use} of any profile (0.90, 0.33), and its charging \texttt{func\_time} (480 min) is scaled by the household's own multi-phone ownership rate due to the multi-generational and hub type of family.

This evening light for Profile 4 is Strict but discounted to 0.80, not 1.00 --- the one deliberate departure from Profile 1's otherwise-identical evening pattern, reflecting this profile's defining trait: households following an erratic dual-residence strategy, absent for unpredictable periods driven by work, family, or social obligation, rather than the settled routine Profile 1's 1.00 assumes. This is a household-level structural discontinuity, not per-appliance erraticism: every VA is suppressed simultaneously during an absence, rather than any single VA's \texttt{occasional\_use} independently encoding erratic behavior

Comparing rigidity classifications across the four profiles (Table~\ref{tab:va-all}) surfaces a finding rigidity alone would not: variation tracks the specific practice, not the household type uniformly. Evening light (VA3) spans the full rigidity spectrum --- Strict in Profile 1, Chaos in Profile 2, Flexible in Profile 3, and Strict again in Profile 4 --- the same practice-type (dinner/homework lighting) classified as fundamentally different in character depending on household structure. Overnight light (VA4), by contrast, is Strict in every profile that practices it at all, regardless of how frequently: a household that is erratic about its evening light (as Profile 2's Chaos classification indicates) is nonetheless rigid about its overnight light once that light is switched on. Duration reinforces this pattern rather than complicating it: the two Strict-classified evening lights (Profiles 1 and 4) share an identical 192-minute func\_time despite differing occasional\_use (1.00 vs.\ 0.80), while Profile 2's Chaos-classified evening light is both the least frequent (0.90) and the shortest (156 min) --- consistent with an erratically-timed practice compressing in duration rather than merely occurring less often.

Occasional\_use itself (Table~\ref{tab:va-all}) shows the clearest separation on morning and overnight light rather than evening light, where three of four profiles cluster tightly at 0.35 despite otherwise-distinct governing rules (\S4.1), suggesting morning lighting frequency is less diagnostic of profile identity than the qualitative rules alone would predict. Charging (VA9) shows the widest spread of any VA --- from 0.60 (Profile 2) to 1.00 (Profiles 1 and 3) --- tracking device ownership more directly than any single governing rule, consistent with the Digital Delegation dynamic (Rule 5, \S4.1) operating across profiles rather than within just one.

\subsection{Energy Demand Modeling: Socio-Technical Validation}
\label{sec:energy_demand_model}


As established in Section~\ref{sec:methods_sim}, two RAMP models are built to isolate the contribution of qualitative, socio-technical parametrization to simulated demand accuracy. The Survey-Based Model represents each household with a single community-wide profile derived only from Stream B survey
data and a hardware audit --- one appliance inventory, two static time windows, and a single occasional-use value applied uniformly across the population. The Socio-Technical Model instead parametrizes each Energy Behavior Profile (EBP) using the Virtual Appliances derived in Section~\ref{sec:virtual_appliances}, so that time windows, cycle structure, and use probability vary with
the rigidity and routines documented for each profile, drawing on qualitative data in addition to the quantitative survey data used by the Survey-Based Model. Both models' outputs are compared against the high-resolution datalogger measures using the Dual-Tier Validation Strategy described in Section~\ref{sec:validation_strategy} (Table~\ref{tab:validation_taxonomy}).

The validation window was fixed to May, the month for which continuous telemetry was available across the logger set and which falls between the community's two principal mobility peaks — the growing season (February–April) and the free-grazing/migration season (July–September) — thereby minimizing the confounding effect of household absence on the load-curve comparison. That this single month is representative for the schedule-bound majority is supported directly by the year-long traces of the settled (Profile 1) households, which show stable system use across all four agricultural seasons (Section 5.1); the seasonal and longitudinal dynamics of the more mobile profiles, which a single month cannot capture, are examined separately there.

Table~\ref{tab:surveybased_vs_sociotechnical} shows results of the survey-based and socio-technical models comparisson. On LCSS, the Socio-Technical Model scores higher for every profile (0.76--0.93 vs.\0.49--0.68), indicating it more consistently reproduces the chronological shape of the day regardless of household type. On RMSE, the Socio-Technical Model wins for P2, P3, and P4 by wide margins (48--58\% lower error) but is actually 23.7\% \emph{higher} than the Survey-Based Model for P1 (0.920~W vs.\ 0.744~W). This is not a coincidence: P1 is precisely the profile where Section~\ref{sec:tier2_results} identified the Socio-Technical Model's own 2-hour peak-timing lag (Table~\ref{tab:tier2_summary}), and that lag carries a real, measurable cost in raw point-by-point error --- large enough that a single, non-adaptive community average outperforms it on this one axis for this one profile. MPDADA follows a similar pattern: the two models are essentially tied for P3 (0.040 vs.\ 0.041) and the Survey-Based Model is closer for P1 (0.410 vs.\ 0.273), but the Socio-Technical Model is markedly closer for P2 and P4 (0.167 vs.\ 2.565; 0.060 vs.\ 1.230).
 
Table~\ref{tab:surveybased_vs_sociotechnical_tier2}'s Modal Peak Hour column shows that peak-hour \emph{timing} is not the differentiator between the two models it might be expected to be. The Survey-Based Model lands within 0--1 hour of the real peak for every profile (average offset: 0.5 hours), marginally closer on average than the Socio-Technical Model's own 0--2 hour range (average offset: 1.0 hour, driven entirely by P1's lag). This is because the evening-anchored peak is a near-universal feature of this community rather than a profile-specific one: a single, shared evening window is enough to place a peak somewhere in the right few hours for any of the four EBPs, so a single average profile is not at a structural disadvantage here the way it is on other indicators.
 
Where the two models diverge sharply is Curve Peak error and MRSD, in the same table. The Survey-Based Model's simulated peak magnitude is close to real for P1 and P3 (14.0\% and 1.6\% error) but far off for P2
and P4 (62.7\% and 39.9\%), because a single fixed peak magnitude cannot represent the wide range of actual peak levels across profiles (2.63--4.97~W measured) --- it can only coincide with whichever profiles happen to sit close to the community average. The Socio-Technical Model's peak-magnitude error is more consistent across profiles (1.7--9.3\%) because it is parametrized per EBP rather than fixed. MRSD tells the same story even more starkly: the Survey-Based Model's simulated MRSD is flat at 0.074 for every profile, by construction --- a single profile has no mechanism to express behavioural irregularity that varies by household type. The Socio-Technical Model's simulated MRSD instead tracks the real ranking exactly (P1 lowest at 0.178, then P3 at 0.256, then P4 at 0.339, then P2 highest at 0.469, matching the real order 0.232 < 0.300 < 0.421 < 0.463), even where it under-predicts the absolute magnitude. This --- not peak timing --- is where the socio-technical parametrization does something the Survey-Based Model structurally cannot.

Table~\ref{tab:surveybased_vs_sociotechnical_ldc} resolves the one apparent exception in
Table~\ref{tab:surveybased_vs_sociotechnical}: P1's chronological RMSE, where the Survey-Based Model
scored lower. Once timing is discarded via the duration-sorted LDC-RMSE, the Socio-Technical Model is
lower for \emph{every} profile, including P1 (0.187~W vs.\ 0.469~W, a 60.1\% reduction) --- confirming
that the Survey-Based Model's apparent RMSE advantage on P1 was entirely a consequence of the
Socio-Technical Model's own peak-timing lag on that profile, not a genuine advantage in reproducing how
much power the household draws or for how long. The P95 column extends the same magnitude-distribution
story already visible in the Curve Peak error: because the Survey-Based Model's simulated P95 is fixed at
4.00~W for every profile, it happens to sit close to the real value for P1 and P3 (4.23~W and 3.90~W) but
far from it for P2 and P4 (2.28~W and 2.88~W) --- the same "coincides only for typical profiles" pattern
identified above, now visible from a third, independent angle (timing-agnostic magnitude distribution
rather than chronological point-matching or single-peak comparison).

 
Aggregate daily energy tells a third story. Overall Mean Power error for the Survey-Based Model is 18.7\% (P1), 107.1\% (P2), 19.7\% (P3), and 38.9\% (P4) --- worse than the Socio-Technical Model on every single profile (0.9\%, 39.0\%, 1.6\%, 19.9\% respectively), including P1 and P3, the two profiles closest to the community average. Unlike peak-hour timing, aggregate energy accuracy does not converge between the two models even for the most "typical" households in this dataset --- the Socio-Technical Model's advantage here holds across the board.
 
The planning-relevant takeaway is therefore more specific than a single sentence about timing. A system
sized using the Survey-Based Model's load curve would place its peak provisioning in roughly the right
few hours --- but would size for the wrong \emph{amount} of peak demand (over-provisioning for P1 and P3,
under-provisioning for the P2/P4 by tens of percent) and would have no way to anticipate which households
are likely to behave irregularly from day to day (MRSD), since the model has no household-level parameter
to vary in the first place.
 
This comparison does not claim that the Socio-Technical Model is universally superior to the Survey-Based
Model: for P1 specifically, the Survey-Based Model's raw chronological RMSE is lower, a direct consequence
of the Socio-Technical Model's own known peak-timing limitation (Section~\ref{sec:tier2_results}) rather
than a general weakness of socio-technical parametrization. What the comparison does show is that the two
models' relative strengths lie in different places than timing alone: peak-hour placement is comparable
between them for this community, but the Socio-Technical Model is consistently better at reproducing
chronological shape (LCSS), behavioural irregularity (MRSD), and aggregate daily energy --- properties
that matter directly for system sizing and demand-side dispatch planning, and that a single community-wide
average profile cannot vary by household type regardless of how well-calibrated that single profile is.
 




\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
 
The results support a claim stronger than the familiar view that qualitative methods add detail:
here, qualitative analysis produced behavioural parameters the survey data could hardly have
generated, and those parameters measurably reshaped the simulated load curves. The clearest case
is the rigidity classification---a single interview-derived judgment (Strict, Flexible, or Chaos)
whose effect is visible in the model comparison. The Survey-Based Model takes a common global
approach, returning a single irregularity value for every household by construction
(MRSD~$=0.074$), whereas the Socio-Technical Model reproduces the real cross-profile ordering,
capturing heterogeneity better. We are deliberate about scope---the claim is not that qualitative
methods are universally necessary, but that for micro-level behavioural parameters in data-scarce,
culturally-specific off-grid settings, qualitative understanding is the input without which the
model cannot be built.



\subsection{Temporal dynamics and scope of validation}
The A/B validation in Section~\ref{sec:validation} was performed against telemetry from
the month of May. A single-month comparison of this kind is bounded in two distinct ways,
which this section addresses in turn using the subset of dataloggers with extended coverage
and the households revisited across successive fieldwork campaigns: first, whether it
captures how differently households behave \emph{at} the moment of measurement, and second,
whether the behaviour it captures is stable \emph{over} the lifetime of the system. The
first is a question of heterogeneity across households; the second, of drift within them.
 
The two extreme profiles illustrate the cross-sectional contrast directly. Across the three
Educational/Agricultural Core (P1) households with long coverage (269, 361, and 368 valid
days), the system is used every recorded day, with no multi-day interruption at any point in
the year~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}). These households form a homogeneous group: their
annual traces resemble one another and their daily energy varies only moderately around a
consistent level. This regularity is the physical counterpart of the profile's governing
rules---the non-negotiable evening lighting anchor and the fixed agricultural routine
(Rules~1--3, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture})---which leave little room to depart from a
daily pattern.
 
The System Breakers (P4) present the opposite condition, and do not present it uniformly.
One household (id~96) is absent for a large fraction of the year: 41\% of its valid days show
effectively no consumption, distributed across 29 separate multi-day interruptions that recur
throughout the calendar rather than concentrating in one season~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}).
A second household is continuously present but consumes at a persistently low and atypical
level; a third is present and steady throughout its shorter coverage window. What distinguishes
P4 from P1 is therefore not that every P4 household is erratic, but that the profile has no
shared load signature: its members are grouped by the social conditions that decouple them from
the settlement's routine---labour migration, dual-household residence, and occupational mobility
(Rules~8, 10, 13, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture})---yet those conditions express themselves
differently in each household. Predictability, not a specific pattern, is what P1 has and P4
lacks. This heterogeneity is not confined to P4: among the Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub (P3)
households with long coverage, one is highly variable day to day while another is among the
steadiest in the dataset~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}). The consistent finding is a division
between the settled Profile~1, whose households are mutually similar and individually regular,
and the mobile and extended profiles, whose households are mutually dissimilar.
 
This annual-scale contrast is independently corroborated by the day-to-day variability
captured in the validation itself. The Mean Relative Standard Deviation (MRSD) measured over
the validation month is lowest for P1 ($0.232$) and high for P4 ($0.421$,
Table~\ref{tab:tier2}), mirroring the settled-versus-mobile division seen across the full
year. The Socio-Technical Model reproduces this ordering ($0.178$ and $0.339$ respectively),
whereas the Survey-Based Model returns a single value for every household by construction
($0.074$) and cannot express the difference at all. The extended telemetry and the validation
metric agree on the same underlying structure---a population divided between predictable and
unpredictable households---arrived at by independent routes, one over a full year and one
within a single month.
 
This agreement is what licenses the use of May as the validation month. May falls between the
two periods of greatest household mobility---the growing season (February--April) and the
free-grazing and labour-migration season (July--September),
Section~\ref{sec:study-area}---and is therefore relatively settled
\citep{CITE-NEW-agricultural-calendar}. More directly, the extended traces show that the
households dominating the validation set behave in May as they do year-round: the settled
Profile~1 households are stable across all seasons, so a single-month comparison is
representative for them. The validation does not, and does not claim to, capture the seasonal
and aperiodic absences of the mobile profiles; those are a property of the annual scale.
 
Heterogeneity at a fixed moment is only one boundary. A separate one is whether that
heterogeneity is stable over time---and the households revisited across campaigns, five for
which an interview was conducted on two occasions roughly one to one-and-a-half years apart,
indicate that it is not, and that households drift along the same axis the profiles are built
on (Section~\ref{sec:ebp}). Two mechanisms are visible. The first is hardware attrition: in
one household revisited after a year, a radio reported as non-functional at the first visit
remained unrepaired at the second, its use contracted to occasional [P90, 2023 \& 2024]---the
temporal form of the Hardware Resignation rule (Rule~13), in which a minor failure becomes a
permanent load reduction absent local repair and supply chains
\citep{CITE-EXISTING-maintenance-barrier}. Attrition is not deterministic, however: in other
households a broken component was replaced, whether purchased by the household or restored
through the field programme [P76], recovering the lost load rather than conceding it. Whether a
failure ends in resignation or restoration appears to depend on household resources rather than
on the failure itself---so attrition cannot be modelled as a uniform decay rate, since the same
fault produces divergent trajectories (Section~\ref{sec:displaced-demand}).
 
The second mechanism is demographic. Household composition is not fixed over a project's
horizon: children reach school age and, more consequentially here, adult children out-migrate,
leaving smaller and older households behind (Section~\ref{sec:study-area}). Because the
profiles are defined by demographic and occupational structure, a household that loses its
school-aged children and working-age adults does not merely consume less---it comes to satisfy
the classification criteria of a different profile, drifting from the schedule-bound Core
toward the flatter, elder-headed Isolated Elderly pattern. The settled and mobile poles
identified above are thus not fixed household addresses but positions between which households
move over a system's lifetime.
 
Together, these two boundaries delimit the model's validity in composition and in time. The
parametrization captures the community accurately at the moment of measurement---across its
full heterogeneity---but a profile fixed at deployment will diverge from reality over a ten-
to twenty-year lifetime as hardware degrades unevenly and households transit between profiles.
Representing this would require extensions the present static parametrization does not contain:
stochastic degradation terms admitting both resignation and restoration, and profile-transition
probabilities allowing a household's governing rules to change as its composition does
\citep{CITE-NEW-load-evolution}. These are directions for future modelling, not corrections to
the present method, whose scope is explicitly a validated snapshot. The extended-coverage
evidence rests on seven households unevenly distributed across profiles (several further
loggers excluded for insufficient span), and the temporal evidence on five households with
opportunistic repeat contact over roughly one year rather than a designed panel; both are
therefore offered as descriptive, evidentiary complements to the validation, not as
independent statistical results (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{System sizing and grid resilience}
\label{sec:sizing}

Off-grid sizing is determined by extreme conditions rather than average ones, which is why a model
that reproduces mean demand can still yield a mis-sized system \citep{CITE-EXISTING-sizing-consequences}.
Four demand properties govern the sizing of a solar system: daily energy, which sets PV and battery
capacity; peak power and its duration, which set the converter rating and instantaneous discharge;
day-to-day variability, which sets the required storage autonomy; and the temporal alignment between
demand and generation, which sets how much of that demand must be met from storage
\citep{CITE-NEW-sizing-methodology}. Each is a property the Socio-Technical Model resolves per profile
and the Survey-Based Model represents with a single community value.

Variability illustrates the distinction. Because the Survey-Based Model represents the community with
one averaged profile, it returns an identical day-to-day irregularity for every household by
construction (MRSD~$=0.074$) and provides no basis for distinguishing a household with stable demand
from one whose demand is intermittent (Table~\ref{tab:tier2}). The Socio-Technical Model reproduces the
measured ordering of irregularity across profiles ($0.178$, $0.256$, $0.339$, and $0.469$ simulated for
P1, P3, P4, and P2, against measured values of $0.232$, $0.300$, $0.421$, and $0.463$), even where it
under-predicts absolute magnitude. Because variability determines the storage autonomy a system
requires, this ordering is a sizing input rather than a descriptive one, and it originates entirely in
the qualitative rigidity classification (Sections~\ref{sec:ebp-classification},~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}).

Peak magnitude follows the same pattern. The Survey-Based Model's fixed peak approximates the measured
value for the two profiles closest to the community average (P1 and P3, $14.0\%$ and $1.6\%$ curve-peak
error) but departs from it for the profiles that do not (P2 and P4, $62.7\%$ and $39.9\%$), because a
single value can coincide only with households near the mean (Table~\ref{tab:tier2}). The Socio-Technical
Model holds curve-peak error within $1.7$--$9.3\%$ across all four profiles. The P95 power level, the
demand exceeded $5\%$ of the day and a direct basis for converter sizing, shows the same effect: the
Survey-Based Model returns $4.00$~W for every profile, matching the measured value for P1 and P3 ($4.23$
and $3.90$~W) while over- or under-sizing P2 and P4 (measured $2.28$ and $2.88$~W, Table~\ref{tab:ldc}).
A system dimensioned from the averaged model is therefore adequate for households resembling the community
mean and mis-sized for those that do not.

Peak timing behaves differently from peak magnitude. The two models are comparable on modal peak hour,
and the Survey-Based Model is marginally closer on average (mean offset $0.5$~h against $1.0$~h for the
Socio-Technical Model, the latter driven by a two-hour lag on P1, Table~\ref{tab:tier2}). An
evening-anchored peak is common to all four profiles, so a single shared evening window places the peak
within the correct interval for each. Part of the Socio-Technical Model's residual timing error is
mechanistic: the model represents each evening block's onset and offset with a single symmetric
variability parameter, whereas the telemetry shows a synchronized onset followed by a dispersed offset
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). A symmetric block with an over-gradual decay shifts the simulated peak
later than the measured one, contributing to the lag. This is a limitation of the current engine rather
than of the parametrization, and it has limited consequence for sizing, since converter and storage
ratings are set by peak magnitude rather than by the exact hour at which the peak occurs.

The relevance of these properties differs between a single Solar Home System and a microgrid. For an
individual SHS there is no aggregation: each system must meet its own household's peak and variability
directly, so per-household heterogeneity maps onto per-system sizing without averaging, and modelling
error in a household's peak or variability propagates directly into its hardware. For a microgrid,
aggregation introduces a coincidence factor, and the simultaneous peak of connected households is lower
than the sum of individual peaks \citep{CITE-NEW-coincidence-factor}. Estimating this factor requires the
profile-level differences in peak timing and shape that a single averaged profile cannot represent;
staggered peaks across profiles reduce the aggregate peak, and a model that collapses them into one shape
cannot reproduce that reduction. The peak-timing information that is of limited consequence for individual
SHS sizing therefore becomes a determinant of microgrid coincidence estimation. The validation reported
here is confined to individual SHS telemetry; the microgrid case is stated as an implication of the same
profile structure, not as a validated result.

\subsection{Material affordances and the modelling of constrained demand}
\label{sec:affordances}

Within the Energy Cultures Framework, material culture is understood to shape the range of practices a
household can perform \citep{CITE-EXISTING-energy-cultures}. The evidence from Raqaypampa is consistent
with this, but specifies it further: the range of afforded practices is governed not only by the
\emph{presence} of a technology but by the \emph{adequacy} of what it provides. A practice can fall
outside a household's effective range even when the enabling device is owned and available, if the
quality of the affordance is insufficient for the task.

Evening textile work provides the clearest instance. Across several households, adult women reported that
weaving and spinning are performed in daylight rather than under the SHS light, because the illumination
is not sufficient for detailed handwork---``I do not do any activity with the light because it is not
clear enough'' [P72]; ``during the day we weave; we do not use the light'' [P74]
(Rule~14, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). The light is present and functional for its other uses, and
the labour itself has clear economic value, yet the practice does not migrate into the evening, because
the affordance does not meet the task's requirements. Provision of a basic bulb is therefore not
sufficient to generate night-time productive activity; the quality of the light determines whether the
practice is afforded at all.

This distinction has a direct consequence for what the demand model represents. The load curves
parametrized here describe demand \emph{as constrained by the prevailing material conditions}, not the
latent demand that might arise were those conditions altered. The absence of an evening productive-lighting
load is not an unmodelled gap awaiting correction; it is a correctly represented consequence of an
affordance that does not support the practice (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). A model that inferred
latent demand from device ownership alone would predict an evening weaving load that the material
conditions do not, in fact, permit---and would size for demand that would not materialize.

Recognizing this constraint is a deliberate scope of the present work rather than a limitation of it. The
distinction matters for planning because the two readings imply different interventions: a latent-demand
interpretation of the flat evening curve would prescribe additional generation or storage capacity,
whereas the constrained-demand interpretation identifies affordance quality---here, illumination adequate
for fine handwork---as the actual determinant of whether the practice occurs
\citep{CITE-NEW-access-vs-quality}. Capacity and affordance quality are not interchangeable, and a demand
model grounded in observed practice under real material conditions distinguishes them where an
ownership-based projection cannot. The same interpretive care applies where a low curve reflects demand
displaced elsewhere rather than constrained on-site, a case taken up in the following section
(Section~\ref{sec:displaced-demand}).

\subsection{Displaced demand: dual-residence and mobility in remote-area energy use}
\label{sec:displaced-demand}

Mobility and multi-locality are not anomalies in remote rural contexts; they are established
livelihood strategies. Seasonal labour migration and the maintenance of more than one
residence are widespread responses to dispersed land, uneven infrastructure, and precarious
incomes across the rural Global South \citep{CITE-NEW-rural-multilocal-livelihoods,
CITE-EXISTING-energy-stacking}. A demand model that assumes each household occupies a single
fixed dwelling therefore encodes an assumption that does not hold for a meaningful share of
the population---and the consequence is not a small numerical error but a systematic misreading
of what a household's load curve means.

The clearest form of this in Raqaypampa is dual-residence: households that maintain a second
dwelling, frequently grid-connected, and distribute their energy-using activities across the
two. The pattern is documented, with dated field corroboration, in five interviewed households
(Rule~10, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). In the most fully resolved case, a household
maintains two separate systems in two houses and relocates entirely to the second for a defined
part of the year (December--June), leaving the SHS dwelling unoccupied in the interim [P76]. The
mechanism driving the strategy is legible in the households' own accounts: the SHS cannot support
the household's full range of desired activities, so higher-consumption practices are carried out
at the grid-connected residence while the SHS home is reserved for basic evening needs. One
respondent described conducting the household's tasks and device charging ``at our other house
\ldots{} where there is ELFEC [grid] light,'' and characterised the SHS dwelling as used only
``to cook dinner, eat, and then sleep'' [P54].

This has a direct and consequential implication for interpretation. At the SHS dwelling, such a
household registers a low, flat load curve---a signal that a conventional analysis reads as low
demand, suppressed need, or an adequately-sized system. The interview evidence shows the
opposite: the demand exists and is substantial, but is metered at the second residence rather
than the first. The flatness is not the absence of demand but its spatial displacement
(Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}). A household following this strategy is not a household
with modest energy needs; it is a household whose needs have relocated to where they can be met.

A third condition compounds the interpretive difficulty, distinct from both displacement and
material constraint. Several households frame their present consumption against remembered
pre-electrification conditions---the kerosene lamp (\emph{mechero}) and its smoke---and describe
the current level of service as adequate rather than deficient [P-Rule16]
(Rule~16, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). This scarcity-conditioned reference frame is
associated with a conservative use of the resource, in which the light is treated as a valued
and finite good rather than an unconstrained utility. The evidence is qualitative and is not
advanced here as a quantified determinant of demand; it indicates only that a low load may, in
some households, reflect a settled sense of sufficiency rather than a shortfall a larger system
would fill.

For demand modelling, the implication is that occupancy cannot be treated as a fixed property
read from a single survey field. The same flat curve is produced by a genuinely low-consuming
resident household and by a dual-residence household absent for part of the year, yet the two
imply entirely different demand and entirely different responses to any change in supply
(Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}). Distinguishing them requires the qualitative account
of household structure that the load curve and the standard survey instrument do not supply---the
\texttt{migration} survey field, for instance, did not discriminate these households, being
common across the sample without identifying genuine dual-residence
(Section~\ref{sec:ebp-classification}). This is why the present model treats structural absence
as a household-level occupancy condition established from interview evidence, rather than as a
per-appliance usage parameter (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}).

For energy planning, the consequence bears directly on how displaced demand should be read in
system design. A dual-residence household's suppressed on-site load is latent rather than
absent: were the SHS location to gain a grid connection or a substantially larger system, the
demand currently met elsewhere could return to it, producing a step increase that a
curve-fitting exercise on present consumption would entirely fail to anticipate
(Rule~10, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). Conversely, sizing a system to the observed flat
curve on the assumption that it reflects satisfied demand would lock in a provision adequate only
for the household's minimal on-site activities. The planning-relevant quantity is therefore not
the metered load alone but the metered load together with an account of \emph{why} it is
low---displaced, or, as the preceding section showed, constrained by inadequate affordances, or
genuinely sufficient---a distinction on which correct sizing depends. Reading a flat curve
correctly is, in this setting, a prerequisite for sizing correctly.

\subsection{Scalability of the socio-technical pipeline}
\label{sec:scalability}

The contribution of this work is a procedure, not a result specific to Raqaypampa, and the two
must be separated when considering where the approach can be applied. What transfers to other
settings is the extraction and translation machinery: the five-source derivation protocol, the
two universal crosswalks that map respondent language onto activation probability and rigidity,
the triangulation of interview narrative against survey time-use and physical anchors to bound
each practice, and the logic by which a qualitative typology is operationalized into a
deterministic classification of the full surveyed population
(Sections~\ref{sec:ebp-classification},~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). What does not transfer is
the content this machinery produced here---the specific behavioural rules, the four Energy
Behaviour Profiles, and the parameter values themselves are particular to this community and
carry no claim of generality beyond it.

The transferability of the procedure rests on a property established during its construction
rather than asserted after the fact. The crosswalks were not defined a priori and imposed on the
data; they were built inductively from the language respondents used, and each bin was validated
against anchor quotes drawn from multiple profiles rather than a single one
(Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}; Supplementary~S1). That a given frequency or rigidity
expression carried approximately the same meaning across demographically distinct households is
what licensed a single universal crosswalk in place of four profile-specific ones---and it is the
same property that makes the crosswalk structure a candidate for reuse beyond this case, since it
does not depend on any one profile's particulars. The procedure's inputs are, moreover, the ones
available in data-scarce settings: interview transcripts and a basic socioeconomic survey, with
no requirement for the metered consumption data that is typically absent in precisely the remote
contexts where demand estimation is hardest \citep{CITE-EXISTING-data-scarcity}.

Beyond its reuse as an extraction procedure, the approach also has a prescriptive value for data
collection. Because each parameter is tied to an explicit, declared source---a modal survey code
and interview timing for a window, the respondent's own account of stability for rigidity,
frequency language for activation probability---the truth-file structure makes visible exactly
which observations a parameter requires. This turns the pipeline into a specification for
instrument design: a subsequent study can build its interview guide and survey around the data
each parameter demands, collecting those observations directly rather than recovering them by
triangulation after the fact, as was necessary here (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). The
present work, constrained to the instruments already deployed, derived its parameters from data
not originally collected for that purpose; the same parameter requirements, known in advance,
would let a future study collect them by design.

Reuse is nonetheless a re-derivation, not a redeployment. Applying the procedure in a new setting
requires that setting's own qualitative fieldwork, its own rule extraction, and---critically---its
own validation of the crosswalk bins against local language, since the expressions respondents use
for frequency and timing, and the cultural meanings attached to them, cannot be assumed to match
those recorded here. The transferable object is the method for building a context-specific model,
not a context-independent instrument that can be pointed at a new community and run. Framed this
way, the claim is deliberately bounded: the pipeline is applicable to comparable data-scarce,
culturally-specific off-grid contexts where interview and survey data are available or collectable
but telemetry is not, and is not advanced as a general-purpose tool beyond that class of setting.

One limitation of the procedure travels with it wherever it is applied, and is therefore noted
here rather than among the limitations specific to this study. The rigidity crosswalk assigns a
single bin per practice per profile, which cannot represent variation in that practice \emph{within}
a profile: where households sharing a profile differ in how rigidly they perform the same practice,
a single rigidity classification necessarily flattens that spread
(Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). This is a property of the crosswalk's granularity, not of
the Raqaypampa data, and any application of the procedure inherits it. Refining the crosswalk to
admit within-profile distributions rather than single bins is the most direct methodological
extension the approach invites (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}
 
The limitations of this work fall into three kinds: constraints on what the available data could
establish, boundaries of the extraction method itself, and gaps in the simulation engine used to
run the model. Several point to specific, actionable directions rather than to irreducible
weaknesses, and are stated here in that form.
 
\subsubsection*{A structural ceiling on self-report, and what it reveals}
 
The most consequential limitation emerged from the telemetry validation rather than from either
data source used to build the model. Day-by-day traces show that evening lighting's onset is
tightly consistent across households and days, whereas its offset---when the light is finally
switched off---varies substantially more~(Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-offset}). Model~B, parametrized
entirely from self-reported qualitative and quantitative data, cannot reproduce this asymmetry:
its variability parameters govern each activation block as a single unit, with no mechanism to
make a practice's start more rigid than its end, so the simulated curve rises and decays with
comparable, symmetric spread. This is not a shortcoming of how the interviews or surveys were
conducted but a ceiling on what self-report can capture in principle. A respondent describing
``we use the light from six to ten'' reports a single modal window, not a distribution over many
nights; the asymmetry between how consistently a practice begins and how consistently it ends is
a pattern that only repeated, independent daily observation can reveal
\citep{CITE-EXISTING-sensor-vs-survey}. It is a concrete instance of the caveat stated at the
outset---that the parameters reflect reported behaviour, not independently validated ground
truth---and one where that distinction has a measurable consequence.
 
Two directions follow. First, fieldwork protocols in comparable contexts could probe
within-practice variability directly, asking not only when a practice typically starts and ends
but how consistent that timing is from night to night, and whether the start or the end is the
more variable---surfacing qualitative signal the present interview guide did not systematically
elicit. This would not fully close the gap, since self-reported variability is still self-report,
but it illustrates a more general point: because the pipeline makes explicit which observations
each parameter requires, it can guide the design of future instruments toward collecting them
directly rather than recovering them after the fact (Section~\ref{sec:scalability}). Second, the
finding motivates a specific engine-level extension, described below.
 
\subsubsection*{Boundaries of the method}
 
Beyond the within-profile rigidity granularity noted in Section~\ref{sec:scalability}, which
travels with the procedure wherever it is applied, two features of the present parametrization are
consequences of limited evidence rather than of the method's design. Interview coverage was uneven
across profiles, and was lowest for the System Breakers (50\%, six of twelve households)---the
profile whose defining dual-residence and structural-absence claims most depend on qualitative
grounding, and which therefore rests on the thinnest evidentiary base of the four
(Section~\ref{sec:ebp-classification}). Where a profile's coverage was thin, some parameters rest
on evidence pooled across its interviewed households rather than triangulated per household; this
is a weaker epistemic status, flagged as such in the derivation record but not eliminated
(Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}; Supplementary~S1). A small number of classifications also
rest on a documented analyst override of the survey-derived assignment, where corroborated
qualitative evidence of a structural condition contradicted the survey fields
(Section~\ref{sec:ebp-classification}; Supplementary~S2); these are logged individually, but they
mark points where the classification depended on judgement rather than on a fixed rule. Finally,
one parameter governing thermal load variability is a declared default, adopted because no data
source directly informs it, and is flagged as provisional pending dedicated measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}).
 
The extended-coverage evidence used to characterise annual dynamics
(Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}) is also limited in scale---seven households for the
telemetry analysis and five for the repeat-interview analysis, unevenly distributed across
profiles---and is offered as descriptive rather than statistical. The daily activity measure used
there is a usage proxy derived from metered consumption, and is therefore sensitive to generation
as well as to occupancy; low-consumption days were distinguished from genuine absence by requiring
multi-day continuity, but the two cannot be separated with certainty from this metric alone
(Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}). Confirming the annual and seasonal patterns it suggests
would require both a larger instrumented sample and a presence measure independent of consumption.
 
\subsubsection*{Gaps in the simulation engine}
 
Two phenomena the fieldwork documented are not yet fully represented in the model, not because they
were not observed but because the engine lacks the mechanism to express them. The first is
household-level occupancy: structural absence is currently applied as a manually assigned,
household-level condition established from interview evidence, rather than through a dedicated
occupancy model. Because self-reports were found to overstate presence, this condition should be
treated as an upper bound on occupancy, and a probabilistic occupancy feature---admitting seasonal
and aperiodic absence directly---is a priority extension
(Sections~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries},~\ref{sec:displaced-demand}). Relatedly, the seasonal
parameter sets used to extend the simulation across the full year are provisional and unvalidated;
the evidence in Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries} indicates that the dominant seasonal effect
operates through occupancy and generation rather than through changes in how present households use
their appliances, so a defensible seasonal model belongs on the occupancy and supply side rather
than in per-appliance behavioural tuning (Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}).
 
The second gap follows directly from the onset/offset asymmetry above. Representing a practice whose
start is more rigid than its end would require decomposing a single physical appliance into multiple
Virtual Appliances with independent onset and offset parameters. Doing so correctly requires a
mutual-exclusion mechanism the engine does not currently provide, to guarantee that Virtual
Appliances representing the same hardware are never simultaneously active and their loads not
double-counted. This is therefore stated as a concrete feature request for future development of
the simulation engine, rather than a workaround attempted within the constraints of the present one
\citep{CITE-EXISTING-RAMP}.
 
None of these limitations undermines the central finding---that qualitative analysis produced
behavioural parameters the survey data could not, and that these measurably reshaped the simulated
load curves. Several instead sharpen it, by identifying precisely where the qualitative-to-quantitative
translation reaches its current limits and what would be required to extend it.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conslusion}
\begin{itemize}
This study set out to understand how socio-economic conditions, cultural norms,
and material realities shape energy behavior in an Indigenous territory, and how
those insights can be operationalized to support bottom-up demand modeling for
inclusive electrification planning. Through the case of Raqaypampa, it makes
four connected contributions.

First, it offers a detailed empirical account of energy behavior in an isolated
Quechua community, surfacing the cultural, economic, and material logics that
govern daily energy practice---from the protected evening study window and the
pre-dawn agricultural routine to weather-driven load-shedding, dual-residence
displacement, and the resignation that follows minor hardware failure. While
rooted in a specific context, these dynamics recur across underserved off-grid
settings and offer transferable insight into why demand takes the shape it does.

Second, it advances a methodological application of the Energy Cultures
Framework, using an extended mixed-methods design to hold cultural norms, daily
practices, and material conditions within a single analytical lens---an
integration rarely carried through to quantitative energy modeling.

Third, it contributes to the practice of data collection in data-scarce
environments. By triangulating self-reported qualitative and quantitative
practices against measured electricity consumption, the study audits the
cognitive ``Say-Do'' gap and shows where self-report reaches its structural
limits, improving the reliability of behavioral demand analysis in remote
communities.

Fourth, and most centrally, it operationalizes qualitative and behavioral
insight directly into a stochastic demand simulation. By disaggregating
identical hardware into behaviorally distinct Virtual Appliances and comparing
the resulting socio-technical model against a conventional survey-only baseline,
the study demonstrates that a single interview-derived rigidity judgment
reshapes the simulated load curve in ways survey data alone cannot---capturing
the cross-profile structure of behavioral irregularity, chronological routine
shape, and aggregate energy that a community-average profile cannot vary by
household type. The comparison is deliberately bounded: the socio-technical
model is not universally superior on every metric, but it is more informative
for precisely the properties---peak magnitude, variability, and displaced
demand---on which correct sizing and resilient system design depend.

Ultimately, the contribution that travels beyond Raqaypampa is not the four
profiles or their parameter values, which are particular to this community, but
the extraction-and-translation procedure that produced them: a five-source
derivation protocol, two inductively built universal crosswalks, and a
deterministic classification logic that together require only interview
transcripts and a basic socioeconomic survey---the inputs available precisely
where metered data is not. Applying it elsewhere is a re-derivation rather than
a redeployment, demanding local fieldwork and local validation of the crosswalk
bins. Framed this way, the method offers a reproducible path toward demand
models that reflect how communities actually live, rather than how top-down
assumptions presume they should---a prerequisite for electrification that is not
only technically sound but culturally sufficient.
\end{itemize}
