\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract
Accurate household demand estimation is decisive for the technical and financial
viability of off-grid electrification, yet conventional models rely on
urban-centric, income-driven assumptions that misrepresent the culturally
embedded routines of remote rural and Indigenous communities. This paper
develops and tests a mixed-methods pipeline that operationalizes qualitative
socio-cultural knowledge into the quantitative boundary conditions of a
stochastic demand simulation. Drawing on a convergent case study in the
Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory of Raqaypampa, Bolivia---combining 50
semi-structured interviews, socioeconomic and practice surveys of 100
households, and continuous datalogger telemetry from 14 Solar Home
Systems---we extract seventeen rigid socio-technical rules governing daily
energy use and consolidate them into four Energy Behavior Profiles within the
Energy Cultures Framework. These profiles are translated into ``Virtual
Appliances'': behaviour-specific loads bounded by empirically derived time
windows, activation probabilities, and a qualitative rigidity classification.
Through an A/B validation against telemetry, we compare a conventional
survey-only model against the socio-technical model. The socio-technical
parametrization more faithfully reproduces the chronological shape of daily
routines, the cross-profile ordering of day-to-day irregularity, and aggregate
daily energy---properties that a single community-average profile cannot
express by construction, and that bear directly on system sizing and
demand-side planning. We argue that in data-scarce, culturally specific
off-grid settings, qualitative understanding is not a supplement to
quantitative modeling but a structuring input without which context-accurate
load profiles cannot be built. The transferable contribution is the
extraction-and-translation procedure itself, applicable to comparable
zero-telemetry contexts across the Global South.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:sample1}

The global energy landscape is currently undergoing a profound transformation, driven by international commitments to climate change mitigation, sustainable development, and the eradication of severe energy poverty. Achieving universal access to clean, reliable, and affordable modern electricity remains one of the defining challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the 2025 Tracking SDG 7 Report, while global electricity access has reached 92\%, approximately 666 million people remain without power, and current trends indicate that 645 million will still lack access by 2030. Furthermore, the International Energy Agency (IEA) highlights that this deficit is heavily concentrated geographically and demographically: over 80\% of the global population lacking electricity resides in Sub-Saharan Africa, and 84\% live in remote, rural communities.
Within this shifting macro-scenario, energy use around the world keeps evolving in time as a product of a broad range of factors that involve external conditions, technological advancements, and personal decisions. Naturally, this evolution is heterogeneous and highly dependent on contexts. As a result, the energy consumption disparities persist, making decentralized renewable energy solutions—such as solar home systems and mini-grids—absolutely critical to bridging the gap for these isolated populations.

Accurate electricity demand estimation is a fundamental requirement for the sustainable planning, operation, and expansion of power infrastructure \cite{adinkrah_artificial_2025, poblete-cazenave_model_2021, guefano_forecast_2021}. It enables the execution of energy efficiency programs and maintains the delicate balance between generation and consumption, a critical necessity given the difficulty of storing electricity at scale \cite{adinkrah_artificial_2025, kavousian_determinants_2013}. This precision becomes especially vital—and unforgiving—in the context of small, decentralized off-grid networks like mini-grids and Solar Home Systems, where forecasting is directly tied to a project's financial viability and technical survival \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023}. When demand is over-estimated, developers install oversized systems that drastically inflate upfront capital and maintenance costs, extending payback periods and risking financial collapse while unnecessarily increasing emissions \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, blodgett_accuracy_2017, herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022, few_electricity_2022}. Conversely, under-estimating demand leads to undersized systems plagued by poor reliability, accelerated battery degradation, and the loss of critical services like healthcare \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, blodgett_accuracy_2017}. Frequent outages erode community trust, force reliance on expensive diesel backups, and ultimately stunt organic economic growth \cite{herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022, few_electricity_2022}.

To avoid these pitfalls, off-grid operators must perfectly align community demand with the physical realities of generation. In solar-powered microgrids, incentivizing daytime consumption when solar generation peaks can significantly reduce the need for massive battery storage, lowering the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) by 25 percent or more \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023}. Achieving this requires a granular breakdown of demand to identify flexible loads—such as water pumping, agricultural milling, or cold storage—that can be shifted to match real-time generation \cite{kavousian_determinants_2013, few_electricity_2022}. Furthermore, it necessitates targeted Demand-Side Management (DSM) strategies and time-of-use tariffs that are intentionally tailored to the micro-level financial behaviors of distinct customer segments, ensuring that newly stimulated productive uses complement rather than overwhelm the system's evening peaks \cite{lukuyu_purchasing_2023, lorenzoni_classification_2020}.

Traditionally, efforts to capture and simulate this household energy consumption have relied heavily on a structural and economic lens, where income, socio-demographic makeup, and physical dwelling attributes serve as the primary drivers of demand \cite{pachauri_analysis_2004, Kowsari2011, poblete-cazenave_model_2021, richmond_drivers_2020}. While budget constraints, demographic footprints, and stark infrastructural divides dictate basic access and economies of scale \cite{jiang_rural_2020, kavousian_determinants_2013, zhao_survey_2019, azam_factors_2015, dominguez_estimating_2021}, reducing energy consumption to a purely technical or economic process critically misunderstands how human beings live. Extracting the level of granular, flexible demand required for off-grid survival is impossible if planners only measure what and how much is being consumed; understanding the "why" behind energy consumption is absolutely critical. As energy literature increasingly recognizes, asking "What is energy for?" is the missing link in historical modeling efforts, which have traditionally analyzed abstract energy rather than human behavior \cite{Kowsari2011, shove_what_2014}. People do not consume raw electricity for its own sake; they consume "energy services" to accomplish deeply embedded social practices and everyday routines \cite{shove_what_2014, day_conceptualising_2016}.

When this philosophical "why" is revealed, it uncovers the cultural, moral, and social values that strictly dictate consumer choices, often overriding purely technical or economic logic \cite{Kowsari2011}. For instance, households may reject efficient electric stoves because traditional meals cooked over open flames are culturally perceived as superior in taste and appropriateness \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020}. Similarly, the acquisition of specific appliances often serves to claim modernity, differentiate social status, and strengthen community ties rather than simply satisfy a utility need \cite{tesfamichael_eager_2020}, while the seemingly arduous task of gathering traditional biomass often functions as a crucial space for social bonding among women \cite{louw_determinants_2008}. These practices are further differentiated by religion and ethnicity, which historically situate specific marginalized groups to rely on certain fuels \cite{richmond_drivers_2020}. Ultimately, what top-down observers might classify as "energy poverty" often reflects a localized, traditional lifestyle, highlighting that energy behavior is a highly context-specific cultural construct \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020}. When planners treat rural users as purely rational economic actors and ignore these cultural functions, interventions inevitably fail; a new technology cannot replace a traditional one if it cannot perform the specific cultural "work" the household requires \cite{kumar_non-universal_2020, shove_what_2014}.

Despite the deeply human reality of energy use, attempts to mathematically model and estimate energy demand face severe methodological hurdles that systematically minimize this human component. Accurate micro-level data is notoriously scarce, forcing modelers to rely on energy-use surveys that are highly vulnerable to self-reporting errors and "social-desirability bias" \cite{Kowsari2011, blodgett_accuracy_2017, m_heinrich_energy_2022, tesfamichael_eager_2020}. While the integration of physical sensors in recent research has successfully exposed the massive biases in self-reported data, telemetry alone cannot explain the complex behavioral dynamics driving those numbers; physical measurements must act as a complement to surveys, not a replacement \cite{rom_using_2020}. Bereft of accurate behavioral data, energy simulation models frequently oversimplify users, treating them purely as rational economic actors seeking to maximize utility \cite{riva_long-term_2018}. This blind spot generates a significant "energy performance gap" between simulated predictions and actual real-world usage \cite{medojevic_integrated_2021}.

This discrepancy is further exacerbated by the contextual misapplication of global models developed in industrialized nations. These models frequently assume a linear progression up an "energy ladder" based on income and uniform power availability, failing entirely to account for Global South realities like informal economies, suppressed demand, and complex fuel stacking—where exogenous factors like unreliable grids compel households to maintain portfolios of traditional and modern fuels simultaneously \cite{stevanato_data-driven_2025, liu_impact_2022, Muhumuza2018}. While top-down macro models obscure daily load fluctuations, bottom-up stochastic models—though mathematically sophisticated—struggle immensely with defining coincidence factors, often either grossly overestimating peak demand by assuming simultaneous appliance use or flattening the curve by spreading loads too evenly \cite{kuster_electrical_2017, herraiz-canete_forecasting_2022}.

Consequently, standard energy demand models heavily rely on top-down, generalized assumptions that fail to capture the complex, highly localized realities of rural and indigenous communities. Because these conventional models ignore the strict boundaries imposed by socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, and material constraints (such as rigid agricultural routines, specific gender roles, and the physical limits of micro-storage), they generate inaccurate load profiles that do not reflect actual human behavior. This systemic inability to account for the socio-technical context leads directly to the inappropriately sized off-grid systems, unmet community needs, and the widespread failure of rural electrification initiatives discussed previously. Ultimately, a critical methodological gap remains: conventional planning tools lack a systematic way to operationalize local cultural realities into the hard quantitative parameters required for accurate, inclusive electrification planning.

\subsection{Study Objectives and Paper Structure}
\label{sec:objectives}

To directly address this gap, this paper aims to investigate how socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, energy practices, and material factors shape energy behavior in rural communities. By exploring these dynamics through a detailed case study in the Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory of Raqaypampa, Bolivia, the study seeks to generate insights that can enhance the modeling of energy demand, ultimately contributing to the development of sustainable, context-sensitive energy solutions.  

The central research question guiding this work is: \textit{How do socio-economic conditions, cultural norms, and material realities shape energy behavior in an indigenous territory, and how can these insights be operationalized to support bottom-up energy demand modeling for inclusive electrification planning?}

To answer this question, this study introduces a novel mixed-methods pipeline that operationalizes indigenous Quechua knowledge into hard algorithmic boundary conditions within a demand simulation model, effectively replacing standard generalized assumptions with localized empirical reality.  

The key contributions of this paper are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contextualized empirical insights: This paper offers a detailed empirical case study of energy behavior in an isolated Indigenous community, uncovering the socio-cultural, economic, and material factors that shape daily energy practices. While rooted in the specific context of rural Bolivia, the findings offer highly transferable insights for similar underserved, off-grid settings across the Global South. 
    \item Innovative use of the Energy Cultures framework: By applying an extended mixed-methods approach within the Energy Cultures framework, the study advances a holistic analytical lens to capture the complex interplay between cultural norms, daily practices, and material realities in rural energy use—a methodological innovation rarely applied in the context of quantitative energy modeling.  
    \item Bridging data gaps through fieldwork and modeling integration: The paper contributes to the discourse on effective data collection methodologies in data-scarce environments. By triangulating quantitative and qualitative self-reported energy practices with actual measured electricity consumption, the study audits the cognitive "Say-Do" gap, bridging demand data availability issues and improving the accuracy of energy behavior analyses in remote communities.  
    \item Modeling contribution (Integrating socio-cultural dimensions into demand simulation): The study operationalizes qualitative and behavioral insights into the a bottom-up demand simulation model, embedding socio-cultural dimensions directly into demand simulations. Through these empirical insights, it is demonstrated how user practices, priorities, and constraints reshape load profiles and challenge dominant modeling assumptions, offering a more grounded foundation for sufficiency-based electrification planning.
\end{itemize}
 
\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{The energy cultures framework} 

The Energy Cultures Framework (ECF) is an integrative, multidisciplinary heuristic originally developed by Stephenson et al. (2010, 2015) to explore the complex factors driving energy consumption and to identify opportunities for behavioral change \cite{stephenson_energy_2010, stephenson_energy_2015}. Rooted in systems thinking, practice theory, and structuration theory, the framework draws significantly on Bourdieu’s sociological concept of "habitus"—the persistent patterns of thought, perception, and action that emerge in response to an individual's objective conditions \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. By treating energy behavior not just as an economic or technological choice but as a cultural phenomenon, the ECF bridges the gap between individual-focused research and studies on wider social and technological structures \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}.

The foundational premise of the ECF is that consumer energy behavior is fundamentally shaped by the dynamic interactions between three core components: Norms, material culture and practices. Norms encompasses the shared beliefs, expectations, and aspirations people hold regarding their energy practices and material assets \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. Some adaptations of the framework explicitly expand this element to include "attitudes, perceptions \& social norms" to highlight both socially governed rules and individual agency \cite{rau_changing_2020}. Norms can act as a profound driver for change, such as when environmental concern motivates new technological uptake, or as a barrier when traditional views limit the adoption of new practices \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}. Material Culture refers to the physical artifacts, technologies, building structures, and infrastructures involved in energy use \cite{klaniecki_applying_2020, stephenson_energy_2015}. Examples range from home insulation and heating devices to electric vehicles and solar photovoltaic panels. Some researchers prefer the term "material conditions" to maintain a clear conceptual boundary and avoid implying a nested "culture within a culture" \cite{rau_changing_2020}.
Practices are the actual activities that involve energy. Practices range from frequent, routinized, everyday habits (like adjusting a thermostat or running a washing machine) to infrequent actions (like choosing to purchase a new appliance) \cite{klaniecki_applying_2020, stephenson_energy_2015}.


A crucial aspect of the ECF is the high interactivity among these three core elements. Cognitive norms heavily influence the choice of technologies (material culture) and the actions undertaken (practices); material culture shapes both cognitive norms and the range of possible practices; and practices determine how technologies are used while simultaneously shaping beliefs and understandings \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. When norms, practices, and material culture align and become mutually reinforcing, behavior stabilizes and becomes entrenched, creating a strong resistance to change—a state akin to self-replicating "habitus" . Conversely, the potential for behavioral change or a "step-change" in energy efficiency arises when one of these components shifts or becomes misaligned,. For example, the acquisition of a new energy-efficient technology (a change in material culture) can force a shift in practices and eventually alter norms \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}.

The core interacting system of norms, practices, and material culture is embedded within a broader contextual environment. This outer ring of the framework consists of wider systemic influences such as demographics, education, household income, available technologies, building regulations, policy frameworks, and energy pricing structures . These external factors are not exogenous; rather, they exert a profound influence on the energy culture (e.g., energy prices affecting practices, or regulations altering available material culture) and, in turn, are influenced by shifting cultural norms \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}.

The ECF is highly adaptive and multi-scalar. While frequently used to understand individual or household energy transitions (such as residential retrofitting or the adoption of solar home systems) \cite{rau_changing_2020, khan_impacts_2020} it is equally applicable to businesses undergoing energy eco-innovations,local community transitions, and even the macro-level analysis of national decarbonization pathways \cite{stephenson_energy_2021}. 

Furthermore, aggregate analyses often mask the heterogeneity of energy users. The ECF is specifically designed to characterize this variability by identifying distinct clusters of "energy cultures"—segments of a population that share similar patterns of interacting norms, practices, and material cultures \cite{stephenson_energy_2010}. By recognizing the heterogeneity of energy consumers, the ECF serves as a powerful diagnostic tool. It allows researchers to cluster populations into distinct "energy cultures" or "behavioral archetypes" based on shared patterns of norms, practices, and material assets \cite{m_heinrich_energy_2022}. For policymakers, this means moving away from a generic, one-size-fits-all approach to behavioral intervention. Instead, by identifying the specific systemic barriers holding a distinct cultural cluster back, policies, marketing, and incentives can be accurately targeted to successfully drive sustainable energy transitions \cite{stephenson_energy_2015}.

\subsection{De-centering the Urban Baseline in Energy Demand Modeling}
The foundational assumptions of standard energy simulation tools are frequently rooted in a Western, urban-centric paradigm of development. These frameworks typically rely on the "energy ladder" model, which theorizes that households act as utility-maximizing consumers, moving in a linear progression away from traditional biomass fuels toward more expensive, modern alternatives as their socio-economic status increases . However, empirical literature demonstrates that this linear model is a theoretical oversimplification \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013}. Instead, households predominantly engage in "energy stacking," utilizing a portfolio of multiple fuels simultaneously \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013, masera}. While energy stacking functions partly as a livelihood strategy to cope with irregular incomes and unreliable supply chains, research shows that fuel choices are also heavily dictated by deep-rooted cultural habits. Traditional practices and taste preferences—such as specific cooking techniques—frequently lead households to retain traditional fuels even when commercial alternatives are affordable . These complexities illustrate that energy choices are not purely economic or efficiency-driven, but are the result of an interactive web of social, cultural, and environmental factors \cite{van_der_kroon_energy_2013, masera}.

Theoretical critiques of this universalist ontology—which often views nature purely as a physical resource to be exploited for continuous economic expansion—argue that it fundamentally misinterprets the realities of Indigenous and traditional rural communities \cite{shokrgozar_fine_2025}. To move beyond linear paradigms of consumption, scholars increasingly draw upon non-Western epistemologies, such as the South American concept of Buen Vivir (Living Well) \cite{gudynas_buen_2011}. As a theoretical framework, Buen Vivir systematically dismantles the modern dualism between society and nature, rejecting the myth of unidirectional linear progress. Instead, it promotes a relational understanding where human well-being is intrinsically tied to the community and the non-human environment. Under this paradigm, energy is not conceptualized merely as a commodity for individual accumulation. Consequently, what a top-down economic model might diagnose as "energy poverty" or "suppressed demand" is instead understood within this framework as a community practicing intentional sufficiency and cultural preservation.

To accurately conceptualize these realities, contemporary socio-technical literature proposes a "cosmologies of energy" approach \cite{mazzone_indigenous_2022}. Through this theoretical lens, Indigenous epistemologies do not view energy merely as fuel to perform mechanical work; rather, energy creation and usage are understood as being tied to profound cultural narratives, social reciprocity, and traditional knowledge systems. When Western energy models ignore these deep ontological differences, they inherently fail to capture the behavioral drivers of demand, risking the imposition of inappropriate technical fixes that lead to technological rejection. Therefore, advancing energy demand modeling requires frameworks that decenter the urban consumer baseline, actively incorporating Indigenous epistemologies to ensure that simulations accurately reflect local cultural realities rather than imposing generalized Western assumptions.

\subsection{Conceptualizing "Virtual Appliances"}
To successfully integrate the qualitative rules of the Energy Cultures Framework and the nuanced realities of localized epistemologies into the rigid mathematical boundaries of a stochastic simulation engine, a fundamental conceptual shift is required. Traditional engineering demand models standardize physical hardware; they utilize generalized probability matrices for generic devices (e.g., assuming a standard probability distribution for a "3W LED bulb" across a population). This approach treats the hardware as a single entity with uniform usage probabilities spread broadly across a day. However, grounded in Social Practice Theory—a foundational pillar of the Energy Cultures Framework—this study posits that people do not consume energy or operate hardware in the abstract; they perform social practices \cite{shove_what_2014}.  

To operationalize this reality within a bottom-up engines, this research introduces the methodological concept of the "Virtual Appliance". While the term "virtual appliance" is frequently utilized in computer science to describe digital twins of smart devices, or in electrical engineering to describe the aggregation of multiple physical loads for grid forecasting, this study reappropriates and entirely redefines the concept for socio-technical modeling. Here, a Virtual Appliance is not conceptualized as a physical piece of plastic and wire, but rather as a mathematically bounded human routine. Instead of modeling a singular physical device, this approach programmatically disaggregates physical hardware into multiple, behavior-specific sociological entities. For example, a single physical LED bulb is theoretically split into distinct Virtual Appliances, such as a "Morning Agricultural Prep Light" versus an "Evening Domestic Cooking Light".  

By standardizing the human practice rather than the physical item, the simulation aligns perfectly with actual behavioral rigidity. Each Virtual Appliance is assigned an isolated, empirically derived hourly probability matrix and a non-negotiable time window dictated strictly by the cultural norms and material constraints of that specific household profile. This theoretical synthesis ensures that the stochastic model respects both human cultural intent and the physical realities of the infrastructure without relying on generalized engineering assumptions, successfully bridging the gap between sociological theory and engineering algorithms.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology} 

According to \cite{zou_mixed_2018}, studies addressing building occupant energy behavior use mainly quantitative methods. However, while these methods hold a solid background, it has been demonstrated that qualitative approaches are needed to understand better social phenomena involving people energy behavior. Furthermore, the use of sensors in research has demonstrated biases in self-reported energy use, indicating that while physical measurements are critical, they are not enough by themselves to understand behavior dynamics and must be used as a complement to surveys \cite{rom_using_2020}. In the other hand, relying on energy-surveys only falls short on predicting energy use \cite{blodgett_accuracy_2017}.

To address this gap, a mixed-methods approach is proposed. Several studies explored the use of it in the context of energy, from technology adoption \cite{university_of_augsburg_adoption_2019, palm_peer_2017} to understand energy practices \cite{rosenberg_evidence_2019, guerra-santin_mixed_2016, wolff_tackling_2017}. Mixed methods involves the use of qualitative and quantitative data, from rigorous collection procedures and analysis, to creative forms of integrating them \cite{creswell_research_nodate}. 

A pragmatic worldview is adopted for this work, under the method of a Case Study. The framework of the Energy Cultures proposed by Stephenson et al. \cite{stephenson_energy_2010} is used. Due to the nature of the dimensions of this framework previously explained, the choice of a mixed methods research design is strongly justified. \subsection{Study Area: An Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory}
\label{sec:case_study}

This study focuses on an electrification initiative implemented across three communities located within the Indigenous Originary Peasant Autonomous Territory (TIOC) of Raqaypampa in Bolivia, Latin America, inhabited by Quechua communities. This initiative is part of a Research for Development project funded by the Académie de Recherche et d'Enseignement Supérieur (ARES). The project distributed 100 Solar Home Systems (SHS) using a co-financing model (68 are located in \textit{Ichuqata} and \textit{Sillar}, on the upper western side of the map, and 32 in \textit{Molle Orcko}, to the east (Fig.~\ref{fig:raq_map}). Following collaborative, consensus-based dialogues with community leaders, standardized systems were deployed featuring a 20Wp polycrystalline solar panel, an 89Wh LiFePO4 battery, and LED lighting accessories (see Table \ref{tab:shs_characteristics}). 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Figures/rap_map.png}
    \caption{Indigenous Originary Peasant Territory of Raqaypampa}
    \label{fig:raq_map}
\end{figure}

Officially recognized as the first TIOC in the country on July 12, 2016, Raqaypampa marked a milestone in indigenous self-determination. Its Autonomy Statute was approved by referendum in November 2016, with over 91\% voter support, followed by the first elections of autonomous authorities in June 2017. Grounded in ancestral values and the principle of Sumaq Kawsay (Living Well), the territory is governed through holistic, culturally rooted practices aimed at managing the land in harmony with \textit{Pachamama} (Mother Earth). Today, Raqaypampa serves as a national reference for community-led governance. The territory encompasses 43 communities across approximately 556 square kilometers, with elevations ranging from 1,600 to 3,300 meters above sea level (See Figure \ref{fig:raq_map}) \cite{oep_estatuto_2016, gaioc_del_territorio_de_raqaypampa_plan_2018}. 

Between 2001 and 2012, the territory experienced a significant population decline, dropping from approximately 10,230 to 7,340 inhabitants. This decrease was largely attributed to permanent migration driven by economic hardship and the absence of basic services. However, according to the most recent national census conducted in 2024, the population has increased slightly, reaching 8,007 inhabitants \cite{CENSUS2024}. 

Household sizes in the targeted communities range from 1 to 12 members. Families are predominantly organized as nuclear, extended, or large extended households, with 75\% led by male heads of household. Dwellings are typically constructed from mud bricks with earthen floors and roofs of clay tile, corrugated metal, or straw. Living spaces are generally shared, with separate auxiliary structures frequently built for cooking with firewood and storing food (known locally as \textit{trofas}). An example of this structural distribution is shown in Figure \ref{fig:typical_household}.

    \centering

    \caption{Typical household in Raqaypampa}
    \label{fig:typical_household}

The local economy relies heavily on rain-fed, subsistence agriculture and livestock farming, with occasional surplus sold in local markets. Livelihoods remain highly precarious; at least 60\% of households report earning the equivalent to less than USD 35 per month or having no direct monetary income. The communities lack access to potable water infrastructure, relying instead on natural springs and rivers that are highly vulnerable to drought. Diets are largely based on locally grown maize, potatoes, and vegetables, supplemented by rice.

Because the communities lack irrigation, survival and economic stability are strictly tethered to the rainy season. This establishes a rigid annual baseline calendar: soil preparation occurs in August–September, sowing in October–November, crop maintenance throughout the rains (October–March), and harvesting in May–June.

\subsection{Multi-Stream Data Collection}
\label{sec:data_collection_analysis} 

To capture both the physical realities of the SHS and the behavioral dynamics of the users, this study deploys a convergent mixed-methods design comprising three parallel streams of data collection (summarized in \ref{tab:data_collection}). Each stream is designed to capture specific dimensions of the Energy Cultures framework  and to nourish the final modeling phase:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stream A: Qualitative Tracking}. 
    Fieldwork combined 50 semi-structured interviews with household heads and key informants, covering daily habits, gender roles, energy-sharing norms, and seasonal traditions (See semi-structured interview tool in Appendix \ref{interviews}), with 50 dated field memos kept by the lead researcher. Each memo is tied to a specific household and records structural and demographic observations, forming a distinct, attributable evidence tier that corroborates the interview material.
    \item \textbf{Stream B: Socioeconomic and Self- reported energy practices surveys}. 
    To capture the demographic boundaries and perceived behaviors of the community, structured quantitative surveys were deployed (See Appendix \ref{surveys}). A baseline socioeconomic survey was conducted with 100 household heads at the time of SHS deployment (February 2023) to capture demographics, livelihoods, and structural constraints. Subsequently, a follow-up structured survey was conducted with 53 household members to systematically map self-reported energy practices.
    \item \textbf{Stream C: Empirical Load Data}. 
    To capture the objective physical reality of energy consumption, continuous data loggers were installed in a subset of 14 SHS households. Operating from April 2023 to March 2026, these sensors recorded real-time, high-resolution electricity consumption data. The primary data extracted were the true baseline wattage and the precise empirical timing of appliance use, which serves as the validation source.
\end{itemize}

The data from these three streams is processed independently before integration (as visualized in \ref{fig:methods}). Quantitative survey data (Stream B) are analyzed using descriptive techniques via Python to establish the socioeconomic profiles.

Qualitative transcripts (Stream A) are coded in the open-source software QualCoder using a hybrid deductive--inductive approach: the three core dimensions of the Energy Cultures framework seeded a foundational codebook, while context-specific sub-codes emerged inductively from the transcripts. The coded data then passes through a three-step analytical pipeline. Code-frequency reports within the Practices category first isolate the high-frequency energy events that anchor the community's physical load profile. Co-occurrence queries run with QualCoder's matrix tools then map these temporal anchors against demographic vectors and cultural norms, identifying which household profiles drove specific peak-demand events and why. Finally, these intersections were translated into analytical memos following a standardized formula,
\[
[\text{Norm/Demographic}] \rightarrow \text{drives} \rightarrow [\text{Practice}] \text{ under } [\text{Material Conditions}],
\]
that expresses each socio-cultural driver as an explicit logic rule. The full coding procedure, codebook, and matrix queries are provided in the Supplementary Material S1. Through this process, the thematic synthesis yields a discrete set of rigid social rules governing daily energy use, which forms the base for Energy Behavior Profile characterization. 

Empirical load data from the physical sensor network provide high-resolution, time-series records of energy demand. The raw datasets are first cleaned to ensure integrity and standardize temporal intervals, after which the measured load curves ware analyzed quantitatively and used for the validation part.

\subsection{Data integration and modeling}
The mixed-methods approach culminates in the integration of the three data streams to create a context-specific energy demand model. This convergence occurs in three distinct phases: energy behavior profiles classification, virtual appliance parametrization and demand simulation as described in following sections.


\subsubsection{Energy Behavior Profile characterization}
\label{sec:ebp_classification}

Energy Behavior Profiles (EBPs) are characterized through a procedure that integrates the qualitative socio-cultural rules (Stream~A) with demographic survey variables (Stream~B). During thematic coding, specific subsets of the extracted socio-technical rules are observed to co-occur consistently within the same types of households, suggesting a latent typology in which distinct rule-bundles govern distinct segments of the population rather than applying uniformly across it. These emergent clusters are first observed during the coding process and subsequently confirmed through the relational matrix analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:data_collection_analysis}, where co-occurrence queries verify that the identified rules systematically appear together for households sharing similar characteristics. This qualitative step is the origin of the resulting profiles.

Once the rules are grouped into qualitative profiles, the variables that characterize each profile are read directly from the rule formulations rather than selected through statistical screening. Because each socio-technical rule is framed around specific actors and social roles, it yields a demographic narrative for the profile, and the actors it names are matched to their corresponding survey fields to produce a small set of structural proxies. These proxies extend the qualitatively derived typology beyond the households for which qualitative material exists to the broader surveyed population, by operationalizing it as a fixed, priority-ordered decision tree evaluated in strict order and stopping at the first match: higher-priority variables
capture the structural factors that dominate a household's routine, while lower-priority variables resolve the remaining ambiguity. 

The tree is applied only to households for which every required field is available across the relevant survey waves---a deliberate scope constraint rather than incidental sample loss. The specific variable ordering, decision logic, and resulting counts are reported with the profiles in Section~\ref{sec:ebp_results}. Missing required fields are resolved through a sensitivity check rather than a silent default: every plausible value for each missing field is substituted and the decision tree re-run. If all combinations yield the same profile, that profile is assigned; otherwise the household is left explicitly unclassified rather than resolved by assumption.

The finished classification is then validated in two independent ways. Qualitatively, a concordance check screens the interview and memo text for each classified household against the thematic markers associated with its assigned profile, flagging disagreements for manual review without ever editing the classification directly. Quantitatively, correlation analysis of each profile's demographic and behavioral survey fields tests discriminant validity---confirming that the classified groups are behaviorally distinct along the dimensions their governing rules predict---as reported with the profiles in Section~\ref{sec:ebp_results}.

Because the classification criteria are entirely survey-derived, every household within scope is assigned to exactly one profile; only the interviewed subset, however, contributes the qualitative material used in the parametrization that follows (Section~\ref{sec:va_parametrization}). Population counts per profile, split by survey-derived and interviewed subpopulations, are reported in Supplementary Material~S1.

\subsubsection{Virtual Appliance parametrization}
\label{sec:va_parametrization}
Once households are classified, the qualitative rules defining each EBP establish strict temporal boundaries on energy-related practices. Rather than treating a household's energy use as an undifferentiated set of generic appliances, practice timing is first reconstructed qualitatively as an \textit{Anthropological Window}: a culturally bound segment of the household's typical day (e.g., the evening domestic block) built from interview narrative, describing what a household is generally doing rather than any single appliance's operating parameters. Each Virtual Appliance's specific numeric window is then derived by triangulating this Anthropological Window against independent survey time-use fields and hard physical anchors (sunrise/sunset). Because the Anthropological Window and the resulting VA window are derived from partially independent evidence (narrative account versus recorded survey field), the fit between them is itself checkable, and reported as a triangulation consistency finding where the underlying data permits.

The translation from qualitative account to quantitative parameter follows a systematic protocol, so that no value is left without a declared basis. Every parameter traces to one of five derivation sources (Table~\ref{tab:derivation_sources}): two are direct technical inputs, one is the triangulated Anthropological Window described above, and two are \textbf{crosswalks} that are classification steps that sort respondent language into quantitative bins.

A qualitative classification of how rigid a practice is -- anchored to an external constraint (Strict), shifting with daily circumstance but describable (Flexible), or unanchored and unpredictable from the respondent's own account (Chaos) -- derives into three separate model parameters, directly for two of them and, via a second-order fraction-of-\texttt{func\_time} rule, for the third (\texttt{func\_cycle}). One qualitative judgment therefore does the work that three independently justified quantitative estimates would otherwise require. This illustrates the paper’s core argument: qualitative insights do not just add to quantitative models, they provide their foundational framework.

The activation probability \texttt{occasional\_use} is derived from respondents' frequency language through a frequency crosswalk. Practices tied to a profile's non-negotiable social rule default to near-certain daily use; where respondents give an explicit count, the value is computed as active days over the stated interval; otherwise it is read from qualitative frequency bins. Every value traces to a dated interview anchor.

Each parameter value is intended to reflect observed behavioral patterns as reported by respondents, rather than to constitute an independently validated ground truth; the degree to which these values approximate measured data is reported as a validation finding in Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}. The complete crosswalk tables, worked parameter derivations with anchor quotes, and the protocol's revision history are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Because the EBP classification is itself survey-derived, every household assigned to a profile has survey data, but only a subset was also interviewed. Each profile therefore carries two population counts: \textbf{N\_survey}, all households classified into the profile, and \textbf{N\_interview}, the subset with transcript material -- the sole source of Windows, rigidity classification, and \texttt{occasional\_use} values. All qualitative parameters are derived from N\_interview and applied uniformly across N\_survey at simulation time; this generalization step is stated explicitly for each profile in the corresponding truth file.

\subsubsection{Simulation and validation strategy}
\label{sec:methods_sim}

To isolate and quantify the value of integrating qualitative data into quantitative energy demand modeling, a comparative validation strategy is designed. The objective of this phase is to evaluate whether socio-technical derived parameters can successfully capture detailed behavior and improve energy demand modeling. In this sense, two distinct simulation models are built with RAMP, each representing a fundamentally different methodological approach for parametrization. 

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model A (Survey-based)}: assumes that, because all households share identical SHS hardware and appliances with reduced space for variation, they can be represented as a single type. A single representative profile is built from community-wide survey averages: appliance inventory follows the standard SHS accessory set; time windows span the average wake-up-to-departure and sunset-to-sleep periods (two continuous blocks); \texttt{func\_time} is the survey-wide mean duration per activity; \texttt{func\_cycle} is fixed to be a 80/\% of \texttt{func\_time} and \texttt{occasional\_use} is a single average. Requiring only Stream B data and a hardware audit, this parametrization is fast and low-cost.
\item \textbf{Model B (Socio-Technical Approach)}: parametrizes the simulation using the Virtual Appliances derived in Section~\ref{sec:va_parametrization}, with time windows, cycle structure, and use probability differentiated by the rigidity and routines of each Energy Behavior Profile (EBP) rather than pooled into a single community average. It requires the qualitative derivation protocol in addition to Stream B data.
\end{itemize}

Simulations are run over one year for each profile of the Socio-Technical Model and for the single average profile of the Survey-Based Model. Two community-level simulations are additionally performed: one for a homogeneous community composed entirely of the single average profile, and one for a diverse community composed of the four profiles in the proportions given by the classification. To evaluate performance, the simulated load curves from both models are compared, at the per-profile level, against the data captured by the physical dataloggers. Because decentralized household load curves are highly volatile and driven by human activity, the analysis relies on a Dual-Tier Validation Strategy that captures not only point-by-point magnitude and temporal alignment but also the macro-level structural variability of human routines.

Tier 1 evaluates the daily chronological alignment of the models. For this foundational engineering benchmark, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) is utilized to evaluate the exact magnitude of deviation between synthetic and measured time-series data, a widely established approach for assessing simulated behavioral variations \cite{kohler_similarity_2022, nti_electricity_2020, adeoye_modelling_2019, widen_constructing_2009}. However, because RMSE heavily penalizes minor temporal shifts in human routines, it is paired with the Longest Common Subsequence (LCSS). LCSS is employed to measure exact shape matching and chronological sequence alignment, effectively accommodating human behavioral elasticity without artificially inflating the error rate \cite{zhang_electricity_2022, kohler_similarity_2022}. Furthermore, to specifically evaluate the model’s ability to capture extreme demand events, the Mean Percentage Deviation Against Daily Average (MPDADA) is utilized to mathematically isolate and assess peak demand predictions \cite{ihsane_new_2018}.

Tier 2 escapes the ``flaw of averages'' by validating the dataset's emergent socio-technical properties through macro-level temporal and magnitude benchmarks. \textbf{P95 power error} is the relative deviation in P95 power---the demand exceeded only 5\% of the day---read from the duration-sorted load curve, capturing the sustained near-peak level that sets converter ratings while resisting both timing jitter and one-off spikes. \textbf{Daily energy error} is the relative deviation in total daily energy (equivalently, mean power), which sizes PV and battery capacity. \textbf{Peak-time spread} is the standard deviation, in hours, of the daily peak instant across the validation period, measuring not where the peak falls but how much its timing scatters day to day: tight for schedule-bound households and wide for irregular ones, so the metric acts as a rigidity signal and tests whether the model reproduces that dispersion rather than only the average peak hour. Finally, the \textbf{Mean Relative Standard Deviation (MRSD)} validates system resilience and behavioural irregularity, measuring the day-to-day structural chaos of consumer routines and thus whether the simulation reproduces households with highly irregular patterns such as seasonal absences or informal infrastructure modifications. Complementing these quantitative indicators, the Load Duration Curve (LDC)---which sorts demand in descending order against the fraction of the day at or above each level, discarding timing entirely---is examined graphically to assess how faithfully each model reproduces the overall distribution of demand levels a household sustains over the day. To complement the analysis, the two models are also compared at the community scale, because sizing decisions act on the aggregate load of all households a system serves, not on any single household. This is a comparison rather than a validation: no aggregated measured data exists at the community level, so the aim is to isolate the impact of modeling household diversity. Aggregation introduces an effect invisible per profile and absent by construction from a homogeneous model: the partial cancellation of non-coincident peaks. This is captured by the \textbf{coincidence factor}---the aggregate peak divided by the sum of individual household peaks---which quantifies how much diversity in peak timing lowers the demand a shared system must actually meet. Alongside it, the aggregate is characterized with the same indicators used per profile (P95 power, daily energy, and MRSD), applied to the summed community load rather than to individual households, together with a graphical reading of the community-level Load Duration Curve.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results are presented in three parts, moving from qualitative pattern to quantitative validation. Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture} identifies the socio-technical rules governing household energy behavior and the demographic variables that statistically distinguish them, converging on four Energy Behavior Profiles (EBPs). Section~\ref{sec:va_parametrization} translates qualitative and quantitative data into the Virtual Appliance parameters used for the RAMP simulation, showing how identical physical hardware is disaggregated into behaviorally distinct loads across the four profiles. Section~\ref{sec:energy_demand_model} presents the results of simulating load curves for the individual profiles and hole communities, then evaluates whether this socio-technical parametrization (Model B) improves on a simplified baseline (Model A) against measured data, across both aggregate accuracy and structural load-shape metrics.

\subsection{The Energy Culture of Raqaypampa}
\label{sec:energy-culture}

The integration of qualitative tracking (Stream A) and structured household surveys (Stream B) reveals that electricity demand in Raqaypampa is not randomly distributed, nor is it driven purely by appliance ownership. Instead, energy behavior is governed by a matrix of cultural norms, demographic shifts, and material constraints, and this governance is legible in how households describe their own routines. Through relationship-based thematic analysis of code co-occurrences, 17 distinct socio-technical rules are extracted. These rules act as the governing logic for daily energy consumption and are organized into four operational themes.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Daily Baselines (Predictable Anchors).} Daily load curves are heavily anchored by rigid cultural priorities. The \textit{Educational Anchor} (Rule 1) dictates that evening lighting windows (18:00--22:00) are protected for children's homework, as one respondent put it plainly when asked why the household first adopted the system: ``we needed lighting to study, before we used candles... the greatest benefit is for studying and doing homework''. The \textit{Agricultural Dictate} (Rule 2) forces extreme early-morning routines to maximize daylight hours in the fields. Respondents describe rising as early as 04:00--05:00 before departing for the day. Evening energy use is further gendered by the \textit{Gendered Anchor of Domestic Operations} (Rule 3), where adult women managing food preparation define the temporal start of peak evening demand. The \textit{Connectivity Lifeline} (Rule 4) reveals that for elderly households whose children have migrated permanently to the Chapare or nearby cities, the cell phone is treated as an emotional necessity rather than a discretionary device: respondents describe living permanently at the homestead while children live elsewhere, and stress how essential a working charge is precisely because of that distance. Complementing this, the \textit{Digital Delegation} (Rule 5) reveals that even where an elder or adult nominally owns the household's phone, day-to-day operation is routinely delegated to the youngest capable household member. Elders disclaim operational competence outright rather than describing infrequent use: ``my youngest son is the one who uses the phone; I don't use it'', and ``I also have two grandchildren; they're the ones who handle phones, not me''. This delegation persists outside the home as well: ``I only have it here; I don't know how to operate it when I go out''. Where Rule 4 explains why a household values a charged phone, Rule 5 explains who actually operates it, predicting that charging frequency tracks the presence of a youth or school-aged resident rather than the presence of the nominal owner.

\item \textbf{System Constraints and Coping Mechanisms.} Users dynamically alter their behavior based on environmental constraints. Under \textit{Weather-Driven Load Shedding} (Rule 6), families proactively ration light and shift cell phone charging strictly to peak daylight hours during cloudy weather: ``a sunny day is a good option to charge it. But when it's cloudy, we... ration''. The \textit{Safety Baseline} (Rule 7), driven not by anticipatory fear but by documented harm from nocturnal darkness, compels specific demographics to leave at least one LED light illuminated until dawn, significantly altering the daily Depth of Discharge (DoD). One respondent grounds this rule in direct lived experience: recounting a scorpion sting suffered while sleeping in the dark, she describes how the SHS changed the household's relationship to nighttime safety entirely:``once a bug bit me, I think it was a scorpion... now that we have light, there are no longer bugs that bite us... now that there's light, we can see; if there's some bug we can even kill it. Before, in the darkness, you couldn't see... our children sometimes step on something in the dark; that's why this lighting is the best thing''. \textit{Infrastructure Mobility} (Rule 8) reveals that families frequently dismantle and transport the solar panels to temporary agricultural camps, causing extended localized demand drop-offs --- a practice respondents name with a specific verb, ``trajinar'' (to haul/relocate), and describe as a known behavior that not everyone engages in: ``others know how to `trajinar' it [move it around]... I don't do that myself'', while another household confirms doing exactly this: ``sometimes we haul it out to the hills, to go work''. Finally, \textit{Nature as a Threat} (Rule 9) shows that culturally reinforced fear of lightning strikes leads users to avoid system maintenance during the rainy season: ``I heard that when lightning strikes it ruins [the panel]; during the rainy season we don't clean it because the thunder and lightning scare us. I also heard it killed people, they say lightning struck the panel while people were cleaning it''.

\item \textbf{Structural Anomalies).} The realities of extreme isolation and unmet aspiration, typical from rural regions, breed adaptation. The \textit{Aspiration Gap and Dual-Home Displacement} (Rule 10) reveals that households with economic possibilities and family contacts, delegate heavy energy use (for modern appliances) to a second, grid-connected residence while zoning the SHS home strictly for survival needs: ``we do our weaving and other tasks generally at our other house... there we also go to charge our phones... but if it's at the other house where there's ELFEC light, we go there to do that''. This is the clearest explanation for why some households' SHS load curves appear artificially flat: demand has not
disappeared, it has relocated. \textit{Protective Gatekeeping} (Rule 11) shows that fear of damaging a fragile, irreplaceable asset overrides communal sharing norms: ``they do come, but sometimes I think they might ruin it, and that's why I sometimes don't want to lend it... they told me it gets ruined, so that's why I don't want to lend it; even if they get upset or angry, what can I do? I have to take care of it''. \textit{Community Override} (Rule 12) demonstrates that routine conservation is temporarily suspended during major community events: ``once the light went out during a wake [\textit{velorio}]. We used the lighting for two full nights and then it went out''. A single account that anchors the model's need for rare, high-impact stochastic spikes rather than a smoothly conservative baseline. Finally, \textit{Hardware Resignation} (Rule 13) captures how minor hardware failures become permanent load reductions rather than repairs, given limited technical literacy and absent supply chains: ``it didn't even last a month; from that very moment it broke''.

\item \textbf{Productive Labor and Multi-Energy Realities.} The provision of basic lighting does not automatically trigger nighttime economic activity, nor does electrification eliminate other energy sources. The \textit{Productive Lighting Veto} (Rule 14) reveals that adult women actively refuse to weave under LED light not from lack of desire, but because the light is functionally inadequate: respondents are explicit that weaving happens by daylight, not by panel light, ``because you can't see well at night with the solar panel's light''. The \textit{Fuel stacking} (Rule 15) shows that weather, not just solar generation, governs cooking fuel choice: households default to gathered firewood because carrying gas cylinders on foot over roadless terrain is prohibitive, but switch to hoarded LPG gas when firewood is wet: ``we generally use a wood-fired stove to prepare our food. When it rains, we sometimes use a bit of gas cooking, very rarely, because it's difficult to go buy the gas''. The \textit{Gratitude/Scarcity Baseline} (Rule 16) grounds a conservative behavioral default in lived memory of pre-electrification hardship: ``before we only had a \textit{mechero}, which gave off a lot of smoke... we got sick from it, because it's a lot of smoke... now this help from the solar panel I really like''. Finally, the \textit{Sedentary Anchor} (Rule 17) shows that physical limitations of aging exempt elderly households from the seasonal labor migration that drives Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8) elsewhere in the sample: ``I live here permanently, I don't go anywhere'', anchoring these households to a continuous, year-round baseline load rather than the seasonal absences seen in mobile households.

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Energy Behavior Profiles (EBP)}
\label{sec:ebp_results}

Before consulting any survey variable, four profiles emerge from thematic coding of the interviews (Stream A): specific rule-bundles are observed to co-occur consistently within the same types of households, rather than distributing uniformly across the sample. The Educational Anchor (Rule 1), Agricultural Dictate (Rule 2), Gendered Anchor of Domestic Operations (Rule 3), and Digital Delegation (Rule 5) consistently co-occur among working-age nuclear households with school-aged children, motivating the Educational/Agricultural Core. The Connectivity Lifeline (Rule 4) and Sedentary Anchor (Rule 17) co-occur among elder-headed households with no co-resident working-age adults or young children, inspiring the Isolated Elderly. The extended-cooking variant of the Gendered Anchor (Rule 3), Aspiration Gap and Dual-Home Displacement (Rule 10), Community Override (Rule 12) and Safety Baseline (Rule 7) consistently co-occur among multi-generational households under one roof, driving the Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub. Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8), aspiration Gap and Dual-Home Displacement (Rule 10) and Hardware Resignation (Rule 13) consistently co-occur among households whose occupation structurally decoupled them from the settlement's routine, motivating the System Breakers.

To operationalize this qualitative typology for the full surveyed population, each profile is mapped to a specific structural proxy, read from the actors and material conditions the rules themselves name, rather than discovered by statistical screening: \texttt{family\_type} (naming the household-structure actors of Rule 3 and the Structural Anomalies rules, Rules 10 and 12), \texttt{occupation} (naming the mobile and adaptive actors of Infrastructure Mobility, Rule 8), \texttt{children\_in\_school} (naming the actors of the Educational Anchor, Rule 1), \texttt{migration}, and \texttt{portability\_shs} (jointly naming the stability conditions distinguishing a settled extended household from a mobile one).

These five variables are applied as a fixed, priority-ordered decision tree to the N = 65 households with complete coverage across both survey waves. This scope constraint is a deliberate design decision, not an incidental sample loss.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Occupation override.} Any household headed by a different occupation than agriculture routes directly to \textbf{Profile 4 (The System Breakers)} regardless of family structure, capturing the occupational actors of Infrastructure Mobility (Rule 8), Dual-household Strategy (Rule 10) and Hardware Resignation (Rule 13) before any demographic criterion is considered.
\item \textbf{Solo/couple structure.} Among the remainder, households coded as Unipersonal or Nuclear Couple route to \textbf{Profile 2 (The Isolated Elderly)}, capturing the actors named by
the Safety Baseline (Rule 7), Connectivity Lifeline (Rule 4), and Sedentary Anchor (Rule 17).
\item \textbf{Educational core, nuclear or stable-extended.} Among the remainder, nuclear families with children enrolled in school route to \textbf{Profile 1 (The Educational/Agricultural Core)}, and so do extended families with enrolled children that are additionally stable, defined as no temporary migration and the SHS kept at home. This stability clause keeps a settled, multi-generational household with school-aged children inside Profile 1 rather than defaulting to Profile 3 purely on family-type code, capturing the Educational Anchor (Rule 1) and Digital Delegation (Rule 5) actors regardless of household size.
\item \textbf{Extended residual.} Among the remainder, extended-family households route to \textbf{Profile 3 (The Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub)}, capturing the Gendered Anchor of Domestic Operations (Rule 3) and Community Override (Rule 12) actors, households whose structure is extended but which failed Profile 1's stability clause, typically through active migration or a portable SHS.
\item \textbf{Residual.} Everyone not captured above falls to Profile 4, alongside the occupation-override cases from step 1, so Profile 4's population combines two distinct mechanisms: households whose occupation structurally decouples them from the settlement's routine, and households whose family structure resolves cleanly into neither the stable-extended nor solo/couple categories.
\end{enumerate}

Applying this tree yields Profile 1 ($n=28$), Profile 2 ($n=11$), Profile 3 ($n=14$), and Profile 4 ($n=12$). One household required the missing-data sensitivity check described in \S3.3.1; zero households remained unclassified. Only a subset of each profile's survey-classified population was also interviewed, and only that subset supplies the qualitative material underlying the Virtual Appliance parametrization in \S4.2: Profile 1 ($n_{\text{interview}}=18$, 64\% coverage), Profile 2 ($n_{\text{interview}}=9$, 82\%), Profile 3 ($n_{\text{interview}}=9$, 64\%), and Profile 4 ($n_{\text{interview}}=6$, 50\%). Profile 4's lower coverage is a specific limitation worth mentioning directly: it is also the profile carrying the dual-residence and structural-absence claims (Rule 10) that most need qualitative grounding, so its parametrization rests on the thinnest evidentiary base of the four.

Independently of the classification mechanism, correlational analysis of the resulting groups' quantitative survey data shows the profiles separating along behavioral outcomes that played no role in assignment, evidence consistent with convergent validity for the governing rules. Family type is strongly associated with daytime phone charging ($\eta = 0.76$ for a secondary device, $\eta = 0.72$ for a primary device), agricultural labor hours ($\eta = 0.67$), and cooking duration ($\eta = 0.56$), all large effects ($\eta^2 \approx 0.31$--$0.58$) consistent with Profile 3's competitive USB-port and extended-cooking signature. Elderly presence is likewise associated with a delayed, flatter morning lighting curve ($\eta = 0.517$, $\eta^2 \approx 0.27$) and, more directly, with a lower count of working-age adults in the household ($r = -0.441$, $p < 0.001$), consistent with Profile 2's demographic isolation. Given the sample size, these estimates should be read as indicative of large, robust effects rather than precise population values, and the working-age-adult correlation in particular reflects household composition as much as behavior.

\subsection{Energy Demand Modeling: Socio-Technical Validation}
\label{sec:energy_demand_model}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:methods_sim}, two types of models are built with RAMP to isolate the contribution of qualitative, socio-technical parametrization to simulated demand accuracy. The \emph{Survey-Based Model} represents every household with a single average profile derived from Stream~B survey data and a hardware audit. The \emph{Socio-Technical Model} instead parametrizes the four identified Energy Behavior Profiles (EBP) using the method described in Section~\ref{sec:virtual_appliances}, so that time windows, cycle structure, and use probability vary with the rigidity and routines documented for each profile, drawing on qualitative data in addition to the survey data the Survey-Based Model uses alone. Both are validated against the high-resolution datalogger telemetry using the Dual-Tier Validation Strategy of Section~\ref{sec:validation_strategy}
(Table~\ref{tab:validation_taxonomy}).

The validation is reported in two stages, matching the two scales at which the models are actually used. First, the Socio-Technical Model is validated per profile against household telemetry
(Table~\ref{tab:perprofile_validation}, Section~\ref{sec:perprofile_validation});
the Survey-Based Model enters here only as a scalar reference point, because its real failure mode is a community-scale phenomenon. Second, both models are scaled to the full classified population and compared as community aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:community_validation}, Section~\ref{sec:community_aggregate}), where the consequences of replacing a diverse population with a single replicated profile become visible.

The validation window is fixed to May, the month with continuous data across the logger set and the month falling between the community's two mobility peaks (the growing season, February--April, and the free-grazing/migration season, July--September), which minimizes the confounding effect of household absence on the load-curve comparison. The year-long traces of the settled (Profile~1) households confirm that May is representative for the schedule-bound majority, showing stable system use across all four agricultural seasons (Section \ref{sec:temporal_dynamics}), where the seasonal dynamics of the more mobile profiles are examined separately.

\subsubsection{From profiles to the community aggregate: diversity and coincidence}
\label{sec:community_aggregate}

Sizing decisions act on the community aggregate, not on individual households, and aggregation introduces an effect invisible at the single-profile scale and absent
by construction from a homogeneous model: the partial cancellation of non-coincident peaks. Scaling both parametrizations to the classified population ($N=65$), the Survey-Based Model as a single average profile replicated across all households (a \emph{homogeneous} community), the Socio-Technical Model as the four EBPs in their surveyed proportions ($28{:}11{:}14{:}12$, a

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support the claim that qualitative analysis produced behavioural parameters the survey data could hardly have generated, and those parameters measurably reshaped the simulated load curves. The clearest case is the rigidity classification. The Survey-Based Model takes a common global approach, returning a single irregularity value for every household , whereas the Socio-Technical Model reproduces the real cross-profile ordering, capturing heterogeneity better. The claim is not that qualitative methods are universally necessary, but that for micro-level behavioural parameters in data-scarce, culturally-specific off-grid settings, qualitative understanding is the input without which the model cannot be built.

\subsection{Temporal dynamics}
\label{sec:temporal_dynamics}
The validation in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is performed against measured data from the month of May. A single-month comparison of this kind is bounded in two distinct ways, which this section addresses in turn using the subset of dataloggers with extended coverage and the households revisited across successive fieldwork campaigns: first, whether it captures how differently households behave at the moment of measurement, and second, whether the behaviour it captures is stableover the lifetime of the system. The first is a question of heterogeneity across households; the second, of drift within them.

The two extreme profiles illustrate the cross-sectional contrast directly. Across the three Educational/Agricultural Core (P1) households with long coverage (269, 361, and 368 valid days), the system is used every recorded day, with no multi-day interruption at any point in the year~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}). These households form a homogeneous group: their annual traces resemble one another and their daily energy varies only moderately around a consistent level. This regularity is the physical counterpart of the profile's governing rules which leave little room to depart from a daily pattern.

The System Breakers (P4) present the opposite condition, and do not present it uniformly. One household (id~96) is absent for a large fraction of the year: 41\% of its valid days show effectively no consumption, distributed across 29 separate multi-day interruptions that recur throughout the calendar rather than concentrating in one season~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}). A second household is continuously present but consumes at a persistently low and atypical level; a third is present and steady throughout its shorter coverage window. What distinguishes P4 from P1 is therefore not that every P4 household is erratic, but that the profile has no shared load signature: its members are grouped by the social conditions that decouple them from the settlement's routine---labour migration, dual-household residence, and occupational mobility (Rules~8, 10, 13, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}), yet those conditions express differently in each household. Predictability, not a specific pattern, is what P1 has and P4 lacks. This heterogeneity is not confined to P4: among the Extended/Multi-Tasking Hub (P3) households with long coverage, one is highly variable day to day while another is among the steadiest in the dataset~(Fig.~\ref{fig:annual-traces}). The consistent finding is a division between the settled Profile~1, whose households are mutually similar and individually regular, and the mobile and extended profiles, whose households are mutually dissimilar.

This annual-scale contrast is independently corroborated by the day-to-day variability captured in the validation itself. The Chaos Index measured over the validation month is lowest for P1 and high for P4 (Table~\ref{tab:perprofile_validation}), mirroring the settled-versus-mobile division seen across the full year. The Socio-Technical Model reproduces this ordering, whereas the Survey-Based Model returns a single value for every household and cannot express the difference at all. The extended measured data and the validation metric agree on the same structure: a population divided between predictable and unpredictable households, arrived at by independent routes, one over a full year and one within a single month.

This agreement is what justify the use of May as the validation month. May falls between the two periods of greatest household mobility and is therefore relatively settled \citep{gaioc_del_territorio_de_raqaypampa_plan_2018}. More directly, the extended traces show that the households dominating the validation set behave in May as they do year-round: the settled Profile~1 households are stable across all seasons, so a single-month comparison is representative for them. This is corroborated independently at the community scale: the heterogeneous coincidence factor, computed over a simulated year (Section~\ref{sec:community_sizing}), falls to $0.672$ during the two mobility seasons combined but sits at $0.756$ in May specifically---close to its $0.761$ average across the settled months, confirming from the aggregate perspective that May is not an outlier month for the schedule-bound majority. The validation does not capture the seasonal and aperiodic absences of the mobile profiles; those are a property of the annual scale.

Heterogeneity at a fixed moment is only one boundary. A separate one is whether that heterogeneity is stable over time. The households revisited across campaigns indicate that it is not, and that households drift along the same axis the profiles are built on (Section~\ref{sec:ebp_classification}). Two mechanisms are visible. The first is hardware attrition: in one household revisited after a year, a radio reported as non-functional at the first visit remained unrepaired at the second, its use contracted to occasional [P90, 2023 \& 2024]---the temporal form of the Hardware Resignation rule (Rule~13), in which a minor failure becomes a permanent load reduction absent local repair and supply chains \citep{shyu_lessons_2023}. Attrition is not deterministic, however: in other households a broken component was replaced, whether purchased by the household or restored through the field programme [P76], recovering the lost load rather than conceding it. Whether a failure ends in resignation or restoration appears to depend on household resources rather than on the failure itself, so attrition cannot be modelled as a uniform decay rate, since the same fault produces divergent trajectories (Section~\ref{sec:reading_low_demand}). 

The second mechanism is demographic. Household composition is not fixed over a project's horizon: children reach school age and, more consequentially here, adult children out-migrate, leaving smaller and older households behind (Section~\ref{sec:study-area}). Because the profiles are defined by demographic and occupational structure, a household that loses its school-aged children and working-age adults does not merely consume less, it comes to satisfy the classification criteria of a different profile, drifting from the schedule-bound Core toward the flatter, elder-headed Isolated Elderly pattern. The settled and mobile poles identified above are thus not fixed household addresses but positions between which households
move over a system's lifetime.

Together, these two boundaries delimit the model's validity in composition and in time. The parametrization captures the community accurately at the moment of measurement across its full heterogeneity but a profile fixed at deployment will diverge from reality over a ten-to twenty-year lifetime as hardware degrades unevenly and households transit between profiles. Representing this would require extensions the present static parametrization does not contain: stochastic degradation terms admitting both resignation and restoration, and profile-transition probabilities allowing a household's governing rules to change as its composition does \citep{CITE-NEW-load-evolution}. These are directions for future modelling, not corrections to the present method, whose scope is explicitly a validated snapshot. The extended-coverage evidence rests on seven households unevenly distributed across profiles (several further loggers excluded for insufficient span), and the temporal evidence on five households with opportunistic repeat contact over roughly one year rather than a designed panel; both are therefore offered as descriptive, evidentiary complements to the validation, not as independent statistical results (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Implications for off-grid system sizing}
\label{sec:sizing}

Off-grid sizing is determined by extreme conditions rather than average ones, which is why a model that reproduces mean demand can still yield a mis-sized system \citep{CITE-EXISTING-sizing-consequences}. Four demand properties govern the sizing of a solar system: daily energy, which sets PV and battery capacity; peak power and its duration, which set the converter rating and instantaneous discharge; day-to-day variability, which sets the required storage autonomy; and the temporal alignment between demand and generation, which sets how much of that demand must be met from storage \citep{CITE-NEW-sizing-methodology}. Each is a property the Socio-Technical Model resolves per profile.

Variability illustrates the distinction. Because the Survey-Based Model represents the community with
one averaged profile, besides RAMP stochasticity, it provides no basis for distinguishing a household with stable demand from one whose demand is intermittent. The Socio-Technical Model instead reproduces the measured ordering of irregularity across profiles, while erring in both directions around that ordering which is itself evidence that the model is tracking real household heterogeneity rather than applying a single uniform correction factor. Because variability determines the storage autonomy a system requires, this ordering is a sizing input rather than a descriptive one, and it originates in what the qualitative approach allowed to capture.

Peak magnitude follows a related but more graded pattern. The Survey-Based Model's fixed peak
approximates the measured value closely for P1 and is moderately off for P3, and departs sharply for P2 and P4, because a single value can coincide only with households near the mean (Table~\ref{tab:perprofile_validation}). The Socio-Technical Model holds curve-peak error within $0.1$--$14.1\%$ across all four profiles, with its largest error now on P1 rather than on the atypical profiles. The P95 power level, the demand exceeded $5\%$ of the day and a direct basis for converter sizing, shows the same asymmetry: the Survey-Based Model returns $4.06$~W for every profile, matching the measured value for P1 and P3 ($4.23$ and $3.90$~W) while over- or under-sizing P2 and P4 (measured $2.28$ and $2.88$~W, Table~\ref{tab:ldc}). A system dimensioned from the averaged model is therefore adequate for households resembling the community mean and mis-sized for those that do not.

Peak timing behaves differently from peak magnitude. The two models are now closely comparable on modal peak hour: the Survey-Based Model is marginally closer on average, and neither model shows an offset exceeding one hour for any profile. An evening-anchored peak is common to all four profiles, so a single shared evening window places the peak within the correct interval regardless of parametrization. The residual one-hour offset the Socio-Technical Model still shows for P2--P4 is partly mechanistic: the model represents each evening block's onset and offset with a single symmetric variability parameter, whereas the telemetry shows a synchronized onset followed by a more dispersed offset (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). A symmetric block with an over-gradual decay shifts the simulated peak later than the measured one, contributing to this residual lag. This is a limitation of the current engine rather than of the parametrization, and it has limited consequence for sizing, since converter and storage ratings are set
by peak magnitude rather than by the exact hour at which the peak occurs.

The relevance of these properties differs between a single Solar Home System and a microgrid. For an
individual SHS there is no aggregation: each system must meet its own household's peak and variability
directly, so per-household heterogeneity maps onto per-system sizing without averaging, and modelling
error in a household's peak or variability propagates directly into its hardware. For a microgrid,
aggregation introduces a coincidence factor, and the simultaneous peak of connected households is lower
than the sum of individual peaks \citep{CITE-NEW-coincidence-factor}. Estimating this factor requires the
profile-level differences in peak timing and shape that a single averaged profile cannot represent;
staggered peaks across profiles reduce the aggregate peak, and a model that collapses them into one shape
cannot reproduce that reduction. The peak-timing information that is of limited consequence for individual
SHS sizing therefore becomes a determinant of microgrid coincidence estimation. The validation reported
here is confined to individual SHS measures; the microgrid case is stated as an implication of the same
profile structure, not as a validated result.

Aggregating the two models to the community scale (Section~\ref{sec:community_aggregate}) lets the coincidence factor stated above be quantified directly. Over a simulated year, the heterogeneous community averages $0.72$ (range $0.56$--$0.83$, $\sigma=0.056$) against $0.77$ for the homogeneous one (range $0.69$--$0.88$, $\sigma=0.030$), with a coincident peak of $311$~W versus $345$~W (Table~\ref{tab:community_validation}). This is the expected direction: identical households peak in near-unison, so the homogeneous model both raises the summed individual peaks and cancels less of them---overstating simultaneity twice over, and, for a microgrid, sizing shared transformer and generation capacity for a peak the real, diverse population does not reach. This is precisely where the peak-timing detail of limited consequence for individual-SHS sizing matters: staggered peaks across profiles reduce the aggregate, and a model that collapses them into one shape cannot reproduce that reduction.

The gap is also seasonal, in a way the homogeneous model cannot express: the heterogeneous factor falls to $0.672$ in the two mobility seasons and recovers to $0.761$ when the community is settled, while the homogeneous factor stays essentially flat year-round. This dip tracks the periods of greatest household absence, and its May value corroborates May as the validation month (Section~\ref{sec:temporal_dynamics})---the same diversity that lowers the coincidence factor is what that section's settled-versus-mobile division predicts, now confirmed through the aggregate rather than the individual trace.

The community Load Duration Curve (Figure~\ref{fig:community_ldc}) captures both errors in one view: the homogeneous curve sits above the reference through the upper fraction of the day and below it in the sustained-load tail, crossing near the 80th percentile. A system sized from it would therefore over-provision PV and converter capacity while under-sizing storage, whose overnight base load it under-predicts by $45.1\%$. This second error is key: base load sets required autonomy, and it stems entirely from the overnight safety-lighting behaviour (Rule~7) the homogeneous profile omits. The homogeneous model thus errs in \emph{opposite directions} on the two quantities that govern off-grid sizing. An effect no recalibration of a single average profile can fix, since it stems from missing household diversity rather than a mis-set average.

\subsection{Reading low demand correctly: affordance, displacement, and sufficiency}
\label{sec:reading_low_demand}
 
A load curve that is low or flat admits more than one explanation, and the three explanations the Raqaypampa evidence shows imply different, sometimes opposite, planning responses. Within the Energy Cultures Framework, material culture is understood to shape the range of practices a household can perform \citep{stephenson_energy_2015}. The evidence here is consistent with this but specifies it further, in three distinct ways: a practice can be absent from the evening load because the technology available for it is inadequate or limited for the task, because the demand it would generate is being met somewhere else entirely, or because the household has arrived at a genuine sense of sufficiency and does not seek more.
 
The range of afforded practices is governed not only by the presence of a technology but by the adequacy of the service it provides. A practice can fall outside a household's effective range even when the enabling device is owned and available, if the quality of the affordance is insufficient for the task. Evening textile work provides the clearest instance. Across several households, adult women reported that weaving and spinning are performed in daylight rather than under the SHS light, because the illumination is not sufficient for detailed handwork---``I do not do any activity with the light because it is not clear enough'' [P72]; ``during the day we weave; we do not use the light'' [P74] (Rule~14, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}).  Provision of a basic bulb is therefore not sufficient to generate night-time productive activity; the quality of the light determines whether the practice is afforded at all. The load curves parametrized here describe demand as constrained by the prevailing material conditions, not
the latent demand that might arise were those conditions altered: the absence of an evening productive-lighting load is not a modeling gap awaiting correction, but a correctly represented consequence of an affordance that does not support the practice (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). A model that inferred latent demand from device ownership alone would predict an evening weaving load that the material conditions do not, in fact, permit, and would size for demand that would not materialize.
 
Mobility and multi-locality are not anomalies in remote rural contexts; they are established livelihood strategies. Seasonal labour migration and the maintenance of more than one residence are widespread responses to dispersed land, uneven infrastructure, and precarious incomes across the rural Global South \citep{CITE-NEW-rural-multilocal-livelihoods, CITE-EXISTING-energy-stacking}. A demand model that assumes each household occupies a single fixed dwelling therefore encodes an assumption that does not hold for a meaningful share of the population and here the consequence is not a small numerical error but a systematic misreading of what a household's load curve means. The clearest form of this in Raqaypampa is dual-residence: households that maintain a second dwelling, grid-connected or not, and distribute their energy-using activities across the two. The pattern is documented, with dated field corroboration, in five interviewed households (Rule~10, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). In one case, a household maintains two separate systems in two houses and relocates entirely to the second for a defined part of the year (December--June), leaving the SHS dwelling unoccupied in the interim [P76]. The mechanism driving the strategy is legible in the households' own accounts: the SHS cannot support the household's full range of desired activities, so higher-consumption practices are carried out at the grid-connected residence while the SHS home is reserved for basic evening needs. One respondent described conducting the household's tasks and device charging ``at our other house \ldots{} where there is ELFEC [grid] light,'' and characterised the SHS dwelling as used only ``to cook dinner, eat, and then sleep'' [P54]. At the SHS dwelling, such a household registers a low, flat load curve. A signal a conventional analysis reads as low demand, suppressed need, or an adequately-sized system. The interview evidence shows the opposite: the demand exists and is substantial, but is metered at the second residence rather than the first. The flatness is not the absence of demand but its spatial displacement (Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}). A household following this strategy is not a household with modest energy needs; it is a household whose needs have relocated to where they can be met.
 
A third condition is distinct from both of the above: several households frame their present consumption against remembered pre-electrification conditions such as the kerosene lamp (\emph{mechero}) and its smoke, and describe the current level of service as adequate rather than deficient (Rule~16, Section~\ref{sec:energy-culture}). This energy poverty legacy frame is associated with a conservative use of the resource, in which the light is treated as a valued and finite good rather than an unconstrained utility and it is common to find among older people. The evidence is qualitative and is not advanced here as a quantified determinant of demand; it indicates only that a low load may, in some households, reflect a settled sense of sufficiency rather than a shortfall a larger system would fill.
 
The three conditions are diagnosed differently but converge on the same requirement: occupancy and movement cannot be treated as fixed properties read from a single survey field. The same flat curve is produced by a genuinely low-consuming resident household, a dual-residence household absent for part of the year, and a household with an inadequate but present affordance, yet the three imply entirely different demand and entirely different responses to any change in supply (Section~\ref{sec:snapshot-boundaries}). Distinguishing them requires the qualitative account of household structure and practice that the load curve and the standard survey instrument do not supply. The migration survey field, for instance, did not discriminate the dual-residence households, being common across the sample without identifying genuine dual-residence (Section~\ref{sec:ebp-classification}). This is why the present model treats structural absence as a household-level occupancy condition established from interview evidence, rather than as a per-appliance usage parameter (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}).
 
The distinction matters concretely for system design, and the three cases pull in different directions. A dual-residence household's suppressed on-site load is latent rather than absent: were the SHS location to gain a grid connection or a substantially larger system, the demand currently met elsewhere could return to it, producing a step increase that a curve-fitting exercise on present consumption would entirely fail to anticipate (Rule~10). An inadequate-affordance household's suppressed load is similarly latent, but recoverable through a different lever entirely: quality of provision (e.g., brighter, steadier light) rather than additional capacity at the existing site, so a latent-demand interpretation that simply adds generation or storage capacity would size for the wrong intervention \citep{azimoh_illuminated_2015}. A genuinely sufficient household's low load, by contrast, is not latent at all, and sizing for a shortfall it does not perceive would over-provision without cause. Reading a flat curve correctly, identifying which of the three conditions produced it, is in this setting, a prerequisite for sizing correctly; a demand model grounded in observed practice and interview-derived household structure can make that distinction where an ownership- or survey-based projection alone cannot.

\subsection{Scalability and limitations of the socio-technical pipeline}
\label{sec:scalability}

The main contribution of this work is a procedure, besides a result specific to Raqaypampa, and the two must be separated when considering where the approach can be transferred and where, even there, it still falls short.

What transfers is the extraction and translation method: the derivation protocol, the logic behind the two crosswalks mapping the respondent language to the probability and rigidity of activation, the triangulation of the interview narrative against the use of survey time and physical anchors, and the logic by which a qualitative typology becomes a deterministic classification of the entire surveyed population (Sections~\ref{sec:ebp-classification}, ~ \ref{sec:va-parametrization}). What does not transfer is the content this method produced here: the specific behavioural rules, the four Energy Behaviour Profiles, and the parameter values are particular to this community and carry no claim of generality beyond it.

Transferability is a property of the procedure, not of the specific crosswalk this study produced. The bins were developed directly from respondents' own words and each was validated against quotes from multiple profiles rather than one (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}; Supplementary~S2), which is what licensed collapsing four profile-specific crosswalks into a single one within this study: a given expression carried approximately the same meaning across demographically distinct households. That cross-profile stability does not by itself guarantee the same word-to-value mapping in a different linguistic setting, where frequency and timing language may calibrate differently; the transferable object is therefore the derivation procedure — parameter-driven extraction, inductive binning, cross-profile validation, and iterative refinement against the transcript — which a new site re-runs on its own corpus to derive and validate its own crosswalk. What makes this reusable in practice is that the procedure's inputs are the ones available in data-scarce settings: interview transcripts and a basic survey, with no requirement for the metered consumption data typically absent where demand estimation is hardest \citep{CITE-EXISTING-data-scarcity}.

Because each parameter is tied to an explicit, declared source, the truth-file structure also has prescriptive value: it specifies exactly which observations a parameter requires, letting a future study design its instruments around that data rather than recovering it by triangulation after the fact, as was necessary here (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}). Reuse is nonetheless a re-derivation. Applying the procedure elsewhere requires that setting's own fieldwork, its own rule extraction, and---critically---its own validation of the crosswalk bins against local language, since the expressions and meanings recorded here cannot be assumed to hold elsewhere. The transferable object is the method for building a context-specific model, not a context-independent instrument. The claim is accordingly bounded: the pipeline suits comparable data-scarce, culturally-specific off-grid contexts where interview and survey data are available but telemetry is not, and is not advanced beyond that class of setting.

The limitations below mark where the derivation procedure, and the model it feeds, reach their current edge. They cluster in three areas: the evidentiary base, the simulation engine, and the scope of what was asked.

The 100-household universe is unevenly covered by qualitative material. Parameters are derived from each profile's interviewed subset and applied uniformly to the rest, so a profile's reliability tracks its coverage, having the thinnest for the System Breakers (50\%, six of twelve households), the profile whose defining dual-residence claims most depend on qualitative grounding, and whose parameters therefore rest in part on pooled rather than per-household evidence (Section~\ref{sec:va-parametrization}; Supplementary~S1). A few classifications rest on a documented analyst override where qualitative evidence contradicted the survey fields (Supplementary~S2), evidencing the say-do gap. The evidence for annual dynamics is thinner and is offered as descriptive, not statistical. Seasonality is the sharpest instance: the data locate the dominant seasonal driver in occupancy and generation rather than appliance behaviour, and structural absence is modelled accordingly by a household-level occupancy mask, but the anchoring absences are too few and too coarsely dated to resolve a household's contiguous multi-month spell rather than its average absence rate. The contribution of this round is to make the missing measurement precise rather than to settle the seasonal profile. Future fieldwork should record absence durations and their timing per household.

Two limitations sit in RAMP, and they are linked. The most consequential emerged from validation itself: evening lighting's onset is tightly consistent across households and days while its offset varies far more This is an asymmetry the model cannot reproduce, because its variability parameter governs each activation block as a single symmetric unit. This is also a ceiling on self-report: ``we use the light from six to ten'' gives a modal window, not a distribution over nights~\citep{CITE-EXISTING-sensor-vs-survey}; its measured cost is only the residual one-hour peak-timing offset still seen on three of four profiles (Section~\ref{sec:sizing}), not a profile-specific failure. Representing it would mean splitting one appliance into two Virtual Appliances with independent onset and offset variability which runs into the second limitation: RAMP provides no mutual-exclusion mechanism, so two Virtual Appliances sharing one device can overlap and double-count its load due to stochasticity. That overlap is bounded and computable, and small enough not to affect the present results, but a native exclusivity (or asymmetric-window) mechanism would resolve both at once and let fieldwork probing onset-versus-offset variability feed straight into the model~\citep{CITE-EXISTING-RAMP}.

Finally, a boundary of what was asked rather than of method. Because the systems here are very small, the most consequential dynamics may lie beyond consumption: how a household builds the capacity to operate, maintain, and extend an unfamiliar technology and how that knowledge is transferred is a question this snapshot raises but does not answer. A longitudinal design pairing load measurement with indicators of adaptive capacity and knowledge transfer is the natural next study, and would also supply the repeat-absence and timing data the occupancy and seasonal extensions require.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conslusion}

This study set out to understand how socio-economic conditions, cultural norms,
and material realities shape energy behavior in an Indigenous territory, and how
those insights can be operationalized to support bottom-up demand modeling for
inclusive electrification planning. Through the case of Raqaypampa, it makes
four connected contributions.

First, it offers a detailed empirical account of energy behavior in an isolated
Quechua community, surfacing the cultural, economic, and material logics that
govern daily energy practice---from the protected evening study window and the
pre-dawn agricultural routine to weather-driven load-shedding, dual-residence
displacement, and the resignation that follows minor hardware failure. While
rooted in a specific context, these dynamics recur across underserved off-grid
settings and offer transferable insight into why demand takes the shape it does.

Second, it advances a methodological application of the Energy Cultures
Framework, using an extended mixed-methods design to hold cultural norms, daily
practices, and material conditions within a single analytical lens---an
integration rarely carried through to quantitative energy modeling.

Third, it contributes to the practice of data collection in data-scarce
environments. By triangulating self-reported qualitative and quantitative
practices against measured electricity consumption, the study audits the
cognitive ``Say-Do'' gap and shows where self-report reaches its structural
limits, improving the reliability of behavioral demand analysis in remote
communities.

Fourth, and most centrally, it operationalizes qualitative and behavioral
insight directly into a stochastic demand simulation. By disaggregating
identical hardware into behaviorally distinct Virtual Appliances and comparing
the resulting socio-technical model against a conventional survey-only baseline,
the study demonstrates that a single interview-derived rigidity judgment
reshapes the simulated load curve in ways survey data alone cannot---capturing
the cross-profile structure of behavioral irregularity, chronological routine
shape, and aggregate energy that a community-average profile cannot vary by
household type. The comparison is deliberately bounded: the socio-technical
model is not universally superior on every metric, but it is more informative
for precisely the properties---peak magnitude, variability, and displaced
demand---on which correct sizing and resilient system design depend.

Ultimately, the contribution that travels beyond Raqaypampa is not the four
profiles or their parameter values, which are particular to this community, but
the extraction-and-translation procedure that produced them: a five-source
derivation protocol, two inductively built universal crosswalks, and a
deterministic classification logic that together require only interview
transcripts and a basic socioeconomic survey---the inputs available precisely
where metered data is not. Applying it elsewhere is a re-derivation rather than
a redeployment, demanding local fieldwork and local validation of the crosswalk
bins. Framed this way, the method offers a reproducible path toward demand
models that reflect how communities actually live, rather than how top-down
assumptions presume they should---a prerequisite for electrification that is not
only technically sound but culturally sufficient.
