SEASONALITY NOTES

**Seasonality in Raqaypampa is primarily driven by the rain cycle**, as
the region\'s food security and economy rely heavily on rain-fed
agriculture (*a secano*) spread across different ecological altitudes.
This reliance on the rains creates a strict calendar that governs not
only farming and livestock but also the community\'s cultural, musical,
and social life.

**Agricultural and Livestock Cycles** The agricultural calendar is
directly tied to the arrival of the rains. **The planting season begins
in October and November**, focusing on staple crops like potatoes and
maize, and concludes around January with the sowing of wheat

. The growing period, which requires constant labor and crop care, lasts
throughout the rainy season until March or April. **Harvesting begins in
March and officially wraps up by the festival of San Juan on June 24**

.

This farming schedule strictly dictates how livestock is managed. **From
June 24 until October, the territory becomes an immense communal
pasture**

. Animals such as sheep, goats, and cattle are released to graze freely
on the crop stubble (*rastrojos*) left behind after the harvest. Once
planting begins again in October, this free-grazing period ends, and
families must actively control and shepherd their animals to protect the
newly sown fields

.

**Festivals, Rituals, and Seasonal Music** Social and spiritual events
are deeply synchronized with these agricultural milestones, serving as
moments of community cohesion and thanksgiving to the Earth
(*Pachamama*).

    > and the start of the Andean New Year

. It is a time for *chhalaku*, a traditional bartering system where
high-altitude products are exchanged for goods from the lower valleys

Furthermore, **the use of musical instruments is strictly regulated by
the seasons**. *Laquitas* with drums are played from San Juan until
July; *Lechewayos* are played from late July through October; *K\'ullu
Charangos* are used from November to January; and the *Tabla Charango*
alongside the *Much\'a Flauta* are instruments exclusively reserved for
the rainy season between February and April

.

**Impact on Social Dynamics, Migration, and Schooling** The seasonal
calendar dictates human movement and exposes friction between local
traditions and state institutions. **Families often migrate temporarily
to regions like the Chapare** to supplement their income, typically
taking short trips right after planting and longer trips after the
harvest

.

However, external systems often fail to accommodate this seasonal
reality. **The formal education system is disconnected from the local
calendar**; teachers frequently fail to integrate the community\'s
seasonal, productive, and festive activities into the learning process,
and the curriculum inadvertently encourages the youth to migrate rather
than valuing their seasonal way of life

. Similarly, **the central government\'s administrative and financial
deadlines are completely \"insensitive\" to Raqaypampa\'s vital
cycles**. State funds are disbursed based on the rigid fiscal year
rather than the local agricultural and ritual calendar, which obstructs
the efficient execution of agricultural support programs

.

**The Threat of Climate Change** Today, this delicate seasonal balance
is under severe threat. The community\'s traditional ability to predict
the weather using bio-indicators---such as observing specific plants,
animals, or stars---is becoming increasingly difficult because the
climate is behaving erratically

. **Raqaypampa is now experiencing shorter, more delayed rainy seasons
and prolonged droughts**, which disrupts the traditional planting
calendar, causes soil erosion, and severely impacts the availability of
pasture for livestock
